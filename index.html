Fermat's Last Theorem, one rung at a time

No Such Form

Twenty-five essays from Fermat's own descent to the contradiction that ends the problem. Each adds exactly one object, and each closes by stating what the argument now has and what it still owes.

The proof of Fermat's Last Theorem is usually told in one of two unsatisfying registers. Either the mathematics is replaced by metaphor — an elliptic curve is a doughnut, a modular form is a kind of symmetry — or it opens at full altitude, with residual representations and level structures, and assumes you already know why anyone would build such things. This collection takes the third route: build the objects in order, and never use one before it exists.

The argument has a shape worth stating up front, because everything else is in service of it. Suppose $a^p + b^p = c^p$ had a solution. Then one could build a particular elliptic curve from it. That curve would be modular, by a theorem of Wiles. Its mod $p$ Galois representation would then come from a modular form whose level could be lowered — by a theorem of Ribet — all the way to $2$. But the space of weight-2 cusp forms of level $2$ has dimension zero. There is no such form. So there was no such solution.

Every essay follows four rungs: What we already have (named by essay number, so nothing is smuggled in), The construction (one new object or theorem), What it buys (a worked example, and the later essay that consumes it), and Reading. Then a ledger — what this essay proves, what it explicitly imports, and what the final contradiction is still owed. The series is finished when the required debt is empty; imported results remain visible.

Several load-bearing theorems are stated and not proved, including modularity, level lowering, Mazur's isogeny theorem, Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich, Eichler–Shimura and Langlands–Tunnell. Part V describes Wiles's machinery without proving it, and says so on every page. About derives the current proof boundary from the ledger data.

Part I · The old method, and why it stops

Fermat's descent in full for $n=4$, then the first case and an explicit outline of the second case for $n=3$. The attempted generalisation fails in a precise way — which is the reason the remaining essays exist.

Prerequisites Essay 01 uses integers, gcds and congruences. Essay 02 adds complex numbers, rings and quotients. Essays 03–05 need field extensions and $2\times2$ matrix algebra; the specialised number systems and representation language are introduced as they are needed. Eigenvalue theory is not required until Part III.

  • 01Descent, and the exponent four Proves: $x^4+y^4=z^2$ has no solution — hence Fermat for $n=4$, and the reduction to odd prime exponents
  • 02Unique factorisation, and where it runs out Proves: the first case for $n=3$ and the failure of unique factorisation; outlines the second case and states Kummer's regular-prime criterion
  • 03The absolute Galois group Builds $G_{\mathbb{Q}}$ as an inverse limit of finite Galois groups and computes $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$ with $\mathrm{Frob}_\ell=\sigma_\ell$; states the Galois correspondence and the Frobenius package
  • 04A new pair of sunglasses: the $p$-adics Proves: the valuation $v_\ell$ is ultrametric, $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ has unique digit expansions, and $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$ is cut out by the determinant; defines modules and free modules of finite rank
  • 05Galois representations Defines continuous $\rho : G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to \mathrm{GL}_2(K)$, reducibility and semisimplicity, and verifies the matrix fact under the trace theorem; states inertia, the conductor, and that traces of Frobenius determine a semisimple representation

Part II · Elliptic curves

A cubic equation with a group law on its solutions. Counting those solutions modulo each prime produces a sequence of integers; the Galois group acting on its torsion produces a representation. Both come back later.

Prerequisites Part I's group, field and representation vocabulary, plus coordinate algebra for polynomial equations. No prior elliptic-curve theory or algebraic geometry is assumed.

  • 06Elliptic curves, written correctly Defines the general Weierstrass form, smoothness and the discriminant; calculates an example showing how short integral form can lose information at $2$ and $3$
  • 07The group law Derives chord-and-tangent addition and an order-five example; states associativity and $E(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$
  • 08Reduction and the conductor Proves: the good / multiplicative / additive trichotomy and that semistable means square-free conductor; states minimal models and the conductor exponents; computes $a_\ell$ for a semistable curve and one that is not
  • 09Division points and the Tate module Proves: the $2$-division cubic, that the Frey curve has full rational $2$-torsion while the conductor-$11$ curve has none, and that $T_\ell(E)$ is free of rank $2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$; assumes $E[n]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$
  • 10The representation attached to a curve Derives: $\rho_{E,\ell}$, that $\det=\chi_\ell$ so every $\bar\rho$ is odd, and that reducible means a rational subgroup; states the Weil pairing, the trace formula, Mazur and Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich

Part III · Modular forms

A function on the upper half-plane, symmetric under a group of matrices. The space of such functions is finite-dimensional — computably so — and carries operators whose eigenvalues are its own Fourier coefficients.

Prerequisites Complex analysis through holomorphic functions, power series and contour integration; group actions; linear algebra through commuting operators and eigenvectors. The spectral theorem is recalled where essay 14 uses it.

  • 11The modular group and its quotientsoon $\mathbb{H}$, $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, the fundamental domain, $\Gamma_0(N)$, cusps and elliptic points
  • 12Modular formssoon Weight-$k$ modularity, holomorphy at the cusps, $q$-expansions, Eisenstein series, cusp forms
  • 13How large is the space?soon The valence formula, then $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ from the index and the counts $\nu_2, \nu_3, \nu_\infty$
  • 14Hecke operatorssoon Commuting self-adjoint operators, a simultaneous eigenbasis, and eigenvalues that are Fourier coefficients
  • 15Newforms, and the meaning of the levelsoon Oldforms, degeneracy maps, Atkin–Lehner theory, and what "level" names

Part IV · The two worlds are one

Two constructions from unrelated fields emit the same kind of object and, on every example anyone checked, the same numbers. Wiles proved the coincidence is a theorem.

Prerequisites Parts II and III, together with the Galois-representation vocabulary of essays 05 and 10. $L$-functions are introduced here; prior analytic number theory is not assumed.

  • 16The representation attached to a formsoon States Eichler–Shimura: a weight-2 newform gives $\rho_{f,p}$ with $\operatorname{tr}\rho_f(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell) = a_\ell(f)$
  • 17Two $L$-functions, one shapesoon Euler products, and why the multiplicativity of Hecke eigenvalues mirrors unique factorisation
  • 18The modularity theoremsoon Conductor matches level, $a_\ell$ matches $a_\ell$; states modularity — Wiles–Taylor 1995 for semistable curves, BCDT 2001 in general

Part V · Wiles's machine

An anatomy, not a proof. These three essays describe the objects and the strategy of the 1995 paper; they do not prove it, and they say so on every page.

Prerequisites Part IV and comfort with ideals, quotient rings and completions. New deformation-theory language is introduced, but these essays ask for conceptual orientation rather than proof-level command of the machinery.

  • 19Deformations of a representationsoon Lifting a fixed $\bar\rho$ to characteristic zero; deformation conditions; the universal deformation ring $R$
  • 20$R = T$soon The Hecke algebra as the modular side, the surjection $R \to T$, and Taylor–Wiles patching
  • 21Where the induction startssoon Langlands–Tunnell supplies modularity mod 3; the 3–5 switch when $\bar\rho_{E,3}$ is reducible

Part VI · The contradiction

Assume a solution. Build a curve from it. Every property established in the previous twenty-one essays now applies at once, and they cannot all hold.

Reading dependencies No new external background. Essays 01–18 supply the logical chain, and essays 22–24 apply it to the Frey curve. Part V explains why modularity is credible but is not required for following the final deduction.

  • 22The Frey curve Proves: from a solution, the curve $y^2 = x(x-a^p)(x+b^p)$ has discriminant $16(abc)^{2p}$
  • 23Semistable, and therefore modular Proves: a minimal model at $2$ with $\Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}$, multiplicative reduction at every bad prime, and conductor $\mathrm{rad}(abc)$; assumes modularity from essay 18
  • 24The Frey representation Computes $N(\bar\rho_{E,p})=2$ — odd primes drop out because $p$ divides their valuation, $2$ survives because $p\nmid8$; and narrows the small-exponent dependency to $21\mid abc$ and $85\mid abc$
  • 25Ribet, and the end States level lowering; computes $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(2))=0$ from essay 13's formula; assembles the conditional ending
