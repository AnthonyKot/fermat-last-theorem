Part III · Modular forms — Essay 14

Hecke operators

A modular form begins as a function and becomes a sequence $a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots$. Hecke operators are the mechanism that makes the best of those sequences arithmetic: their coefficients become eigenvalues, and multiplication of indices becomes multiplication of numbers.

This essay proves one step and imports one machine Defines: the Hecke operators $T_n$ for indices coprime to the level. Proves: their $q$-coefficient formula, that a normalised eigenform has $\lambda_n=a_n$, and the resulting multiplicative and prime-power relations. Imports: the Petersson inner product and self-adjointness, which together with the spectral theorem give simultaneous eigenspaces. Ledger: Resolves L11 by proof plus registered import.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essay 12 changed the object of study. A cusp form is still a holomorphic function on $\mathbb H$, but at the cusp it is a series

$$ f(z)=\sum_{r\geq1}a_rq^r,\qquad q=e^{2\pi iz}. $$

That essay exhibited two sequences with a property their definition never asked for. Ramanujan's coefficients of $\Delta$ are multiplicative, and so are the coefficients of

$$ f_{11}(z)=\bigl(\eta(z)\eta(11z)\bigr)^2 =q-2q^2-q^3+2q^4+q^5+2q^6-2q^7+\cdots. $$

For example $a_6=a_2a_3=2$ and $a_{10}=a_2a_5=-2$. Why should coefficients produced by expanding an infinite product know that $6=2\cdot3$?

Essay 13 supplies the other half of the setup: $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ is finite-dimensional, and in the case that has followed us through the book,

$$ \dim S_2(\Gamma_0(11))=1. $$

Finite-dimensionality lets linear algebra enter. The aim is to build natural linear operators on this space, find common eigenvectors, and discover that the corresponding eigenvalues have been sitting in the $q$-expansion all along.

The scope fence: only indices coprime to the level. At level $N\gt1$, the clean operators in this essay are $T_n$ with $\gcd(n,N)=1$. At primes dividing $N$ the definition changes, self-adjointness can fail, and the formulas change. Saying that every form behaves perfectly under every $T_n$ would erase exactly the complication that newform theory exists to solve. Essay 15 takes over at that boundary.

Rung 2 The construction

An average over neighbouring lattices

Essay 07 wrote a complex elliptic curve as $\mathbb C/\Lambda$. A sublattice $\Lambda'\subset\Lambda$ of index $n$ gives an isogeny between the corresponding complex tori. The geometric Hecke operation sends a lattice to the formal sum of all its index-$n$ sublattices:

$$ T_n[\Lambda]=\sum_{[\Lambda:\Lambda']=n}[\Lambda']. $$

So $T_n$ is an averaging operation over all degree-$n$ neighbours. This is the first reason it belongs in the elliptic-curve story rather than being an arbitrary trick on power series: modular forms and elliptic curves meet at the level of isogeny classes, and Hecke operators already average over isogenies.

For a level-one form of weight $k$, writing the index-$n$ sublattices explicitly turns that geometric operation into

$$ (T_nf)(z) =n^{k-1}\sum_{\substack{ad=n\\a,d\gt0}}d^{-k} \sum_{b=0}^{d-1}f\left(\frac{az+b}{d}\right). \tag{1} $$

For $\Gamma_0(N)$ the construction has to remember the level structure. When $\gcd(n,N)=1$, it preserves $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ and has the same coefficient formula derived below. That preservation statement is part of the standard Hecke construction; what this essay computes is its effect on the sequence $(a_r)$.

The prime calculation

Take a prime $\ell\nmid N$. Formula (1) has just two kinds of terms:

$$ (T_\ell f)(z)=\ell^{k-1}f(\ell z) +\frac1\ell\sum_{b=0}^{\ell-1}f\left(\frac{z+b}{\ell}\right). \tag{2} $$

The first term replaces $q^r$ by $q^{\ell r}$. In the second, a term $a_rq^r$ acquires the factor $\zeta_\ell^{br}$, where $\zeta_\ell=e^{2\pi i/\ell}$. Averaging over $b$ uses the root-of-unity filter

$$ \frac1\ell\sum_{b=0}^{\ell-1}\zeta_\ell^{br} = \begin{cases} 1,&\ell\mid r,\\ 0,&\ell\nmid r. \end{cases} $$

Only coefficients whose index is divisible by $\ell$ survive. Reading the coefficient of $q^r$ in (2) gives

$$ \boxed{\quad a_r(T_\ell f)=a_{\ell r}(f)+\ell^{k-1}a_{r/\ell}(f), \quad} \tag{3} $$

where $a_{r/\ell}=0$ when $\ell\nmid r$. The two terms remember the two directions in (2): one pulls a high-index coefficient down; the other pushes a low-index coefficient up.

The same calculation for arbitrary $n$ yields the divisor-sum form

$$ \boxed{\quad a_r(T_nf)= \sum_{d\mid\gcd(r,n)}d^{k-1}a_{rn/d^2}(f), \qquad \gcd(n,N)=1. \quad} \tag{4} $$

There is no analysis left in (4). Once $f$ is known by its coefficients, $T_nf$ is computed by divisors and integer arithmetic.

Why the operators commute

For coprime $m$ and $n$, apply (4) twice. Every divisor of $\gcd(r,mn)$ splits uniquely as $d_1d_2$ with $d_1\mid\gcd(r,m)$ and $d_2\mid\gcd(r,n)$. The two nested sums therefore become the single sum for $T_{mn}$:

$$ T_mT_n=T_{mn}=T_nT_m \qquad\bigl(\gcd(m,n)=1,\ \gcd(mn,N)=1\bigr). $$

For powers of a good prime the corresponding relation is

$$ T_{\ell^{s+1}} =T_\ell T_{\ell^s}-\ell^{k-1}T_{\ell^{s-1}} \qquad(s\geq1). \tag{5} $$

Together these relations imply that all the good Hecke operators commute. A countable family of commuting operators is waiting for the spectral theorem — but it still needs an inner product with respect to which the operators are Hermitian.

The imported analytic machine

The required inner product is the Petersson inner product. For cusp forms $f,g\in S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$,

$$ \langle f,g\rangle =\int_{\Gamma_0(N)\backslash\mathbb H} f(z)\overline{g(z)}\,y^{k-2}\,dx\,dy, \qquad z=x+iy. $$

The weight factors cancel the transformation of $y$ and the hyperbolic measure, while vanishing at the cusps makes the integral converge. The result is a positive-definite Hermitian form.

Imported: Petersson self-adjointness. For every $n$ coprime to $N$, $T_n$ preserves $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ and is self-adjoint for the Petersson product: $$\langle T_nf,g\rangle=\langle f,T_ng\rangle.$$ Proving this properly requires the double-coset theory this collection does not otherwise use. We import it, rather than turning one rung of the FLT chain into a course in automorphic forms.

Now cash the linear-algebra prerequisite. A Hermitian operator is diagonalisable with an orthonormal eigenbasis. If another operator commutes with it, it preserves each eigenspace, so it can be diagonalised inside those smaller spaces. Repeat. Since the ambient space is finite-dimensional, the refinement stops, giving a decomposition into simultaneous eigenspaces for every $T_n$ with $\gcd(n,N)=1$.

There is an important omission in that sentence: at level $N\gt1$, those common eigenspaces need not be one-dimensional. The spectral theorem gives simultaneous eigenspaces; it does not yet give a canonical normalised basis. That is the precise problem essay 15 solves.

The eigenvalues were coefficients

Suppose $f=\sum_{r\geq1}a_rq^r$ is a simultaneous eigenform for the good operators and $a_1\neq0$. Rescale it so that $a_1=1$; this is what normalised means. If

$$ T_nf=\lambda_nf, $$

compare the coefficient of $q$. Formula (4) has only $d=1$ when $r=1$, so

$$ a_1(T_nf)=a_n(f), \qquad a_1(\lambda_nf)=\lambda_na_1=\lambda_n. $$

Therefore

$$ \boxed{\quad\lambda_n=a_n \qquad\text{for every }\gcd(n,N)=1.\quad} \tag{6} $$

This is the load-bearing conclusion. The notation $a_\ell(f)$ in Eichler–Shimura and the Modularity Theorem is not just “the coefficient someone happened to print.” It is the eigenvalue of a canonical geometric operator.

Multiplicativity falls out

Let $m$ and $n$ be coprime to each other and to $N$. Apply the coprime operator relation to a normalised eigenform:

$$ a_{mn}f=T_{mn}f=T_mT_nf=T_m(a_nf)=a_na_mf. $$

Since $f\neq0$,

$$ \boxed{\quad a_{mn}=a_ma_n \qquad\bigl(\gcd(m,n)=1,\ \gcd(mn,N)=1\bigr).\quad} \tag{7} $$

And (5) gives, at a prime $\ell\nmid N$,

$$ \boxed{\quad a_{\ell^{s+1}} =a_\ell a_{\ell^s}-\ell^{k-1}a_{\ell^{s-1}}. \quad} \tag{8} $$

Equations (7) and (8) say that every good coefficient is determined by the coefficients $a_\ell$ at good primes. The infinite sequence has collapsed to prime-indexed data.

Rung 3 What it buys

The level-11 form has nowhere else to go

Return to $f_{11}$. Essay 13 proved that $S_2(\Gamma_0(11))$ is one-dimensional. Every good $T_n$ preserves that line, so any nonzero vector on it is automatically an eigenvector. Since $a_1(f_{11})=1$, equation (6) says that the eigenvalue of $T_n$ is exactly $a_n$ for every $n$ coprime to $11$.

For $T_2$, formula (3) makes the first four coefficients visible:

Thus $T_2f_{11}=-2f_{11}$, with eigenvalue $-2=a_2$. Likewise $T_3f_{11}=-f_{11}$ and $T_5f_{11}=f_{11}$. The multiplicativity observed in essay 12 is no longer an unexplained pattern:

$$ a_6=a_2a_3=2,\qquad a_{10}=a_2a_5=-2,\qquad a_{15}=a_3a_5=-1. $$

The prime-power recurrence is different from ordinary complete multiplicativity. At weight two,

$$ a_4=a_2^2-2a_1=4-2=2,\qquad a_8=a_2a_4-2a_2=-4+4=0. $$

That correction term $\ell^{k-1}$ is what will become the quadratic term in an Euler factor in essay 17.

The first unmistakable clue

Essay 08 attached numbers $a_\ell(E)=\ell+1-\#E(\mathbb F_\ell)$ to an elliptic curve. Essay 10 made them traces of Frobenius. This essay has now attached numbers $a_\ell(f)$ to a modular form as eigenvalues of Hecke operators. The constructions share no ingredients:

Yet for the conductor-$11$ curve and $f_{11}$, essay 12 computed that the two columns give the same integers. Even the prime-power recurrence (8), at weight two, has the same shape as the recurrence for Frobenius traces over $\mathbb F_{\ell^s}$. This does not prove modularity. It is evidence that the two worlds are emitting the same arithmetic signal.

Exactly where this essay stops

Three limitations remain, and each becomes work for a later essay.

  • The bad primes. The formula used here is not the operator at $\ell\mid N$. If one deliberately applies (3) at $\ell=11$ to $f_{11}$, the resulting series is not $a_{11}f_{11}$. The verification script checks this failure rather than hiding it.
  • Common eigenspaces can be larger than one dimension. At higher level, lower-level forms can appear more than once. The good operators see the same eigenvalues on those copies, so the spectral theorem alone cannot distinguish them.
  • The level is not yet intrinsic. A function modular at level $M$ is also modular at multiples of $M$. Calling $N$ “the level” therefore needs a minimality theory, not merely membership in $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$.

Essay 15 resolves all three with oldforms, degeneracy maps, the new subspace, and the bad-prime operators. Its normalised newforms are the one-dimensional eigenlines that essays 16–18 can attach representations and $L$-functions to.

The downstream payload

  • Essay 15 turns the simultaneous good eigenspaces into canonical newforms with an honest level.
  • Essay 16 attaches a Galois representation whose Frobenius trace is the Hecke eigenvalue $a_\ell(f)$.
  • Essay 17 converts (7) and (8) into an Euler product.
  • Essay 18 can finally state modularity as the equality $a_\ell(E)=a_\ell(f)$, conductor matching level.

Rung 4 Reading

The coefficient calculation and eigenforms. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 24, theorem 24.11 and corollaries 24.12–24.15, gives the Hecke relations, computes $a_r(T_\ell f)=a_{\ell r}+\ell^{k-1}a_{r/\ell}$, and proves $a_1(T_nf)=a_n(f)$. Remark 24.16 states the exact restriction used here: at level $N$ these formulas hold for $n$ coprime to $N$, while the operators at primes dividing $N$ must be modified.

The imported eigenbasis mechanism. The same lecture defines the Petersson product in definition 24.18, recalls simultaneous diagonalisation in lemma 24.19, and proves the level-one eigenbasis statement in theorem 24.20. Its discussion immediately after that theorem explains both failures at general level: only the prime-to-level operators are Hermitian, and their simultaneous eigenspaces need not be one-dimensional. Theorem 24.21 is the newform repair, reserved for essay 15.

Further reference. Diamond and Shurman's A First Course in Modular Forms develops Hecke operators through double cosets, the Petersson product, self-adjointness and the newform decomposition.
