Part I · The old method, and why it stops — Essay 04

A new pair of sunglasses: the $p$-adics

Essays 01 and 02 already counted how many times a prime divided things. Give that counting function a name, decide that a number is small when the count is large, and a second notion of distance appears on the rational numbers — the one every representation in this collection is written in.

This essay proves, defines, and states Proves: that $v_\ell$ is additive on products and ultrametric on sums, that every $\ell$-adic integer has a unique digit expansion, and that a matrix over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ is invertible there exactly when its determinant is a unit. Defines: $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$, $\mathbb{Q}_\ell$, modules, free modules of finite rank. States: that $\mathbb{Q}_\ell$ is the completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ for $|\cdot|_\ell$.

Rung 1 What we already have

This essay stands on 01 and 02 alone. It does not use the Galois theory of essay 03, and a reader who wants the number systems before the symmetry groups can take these two essays in either order.

What it does use is a habit both earlier essays already have. In essay 01 the descent turned on a step of this shape: $a$, $b$ and $a^2+b^2$ are pairwise coprime and their product is a square, therefore each factor is separately a square. In essay 02 the whole first case for $n=3$ came down to counting factors of $\lambda$ — the cubes were $\pm1$ modulo $\lambda^4$, and the contradiction was that $3$ carries only two factors of $\lambda$ when four were needed.

Both arguments are exponent bookkeeping. Neither essay gave the bookkeeping a name, and that is the omission this essay repairs. Naming it does more than tidy up: it turns a collection of divisibility tricks into a number system, and that number system is where $\mathrm{GL}_2$ will act.

We also need one piece of vocabulary the reader may not have met, since it is not quite in the standard first course: a module. It appears in Rung 2's second half.

A notation convention, fixed here and used for the rest of the collection. Two different primes are in play from now on, and conflating them is the easiest way to misread the endgame.
  • $p$ is reserved for the Fermat exponent — the prime in $a^p+b^p=c^p$, fixed once a hypothetical solution is assumed.
  • $\ell$ denotes an auxiliary prime, one we are free to vary: the prime we reduce a curve modulo, or take a valuation at.
Everything in this essay is stated for a general $\ell$, and so is everything in essays 08 through 10. The two letters collide at exactly one point, and it is the point of the whole argument: the Frey construction takes the auxiliary prime to be the Fermat exponent itself, $\ell=p$. So $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $\bar\rho_{E,p}$ in the later essays are the general $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ and $\bar\rho_{E,\ell}$ specialised, not different objects.

Rung 2 The construction

The valuation, and a second notion of size

Fix a prime $\ell$. Every non-zero rational number $x$ can be written $x=\ell^{e}\cdot u$ where $u$ is a ratio of integers with neither numerator nor denominator divisible by $\ell$, and the exponent $e$ is determined by $x$. Define the $\ell$-adic valuation

$$ v_\ell(x)=e,\qquad v_\ell(0)=+\infty. $$

So $v_2(12)=2$, $v_3(12)=1$, and $v_3(1/9)=-2$. Two properties follow immediately from unique factorisation in $\mathbb{Z}$:

$$ v_\ell(xy)=v_\ell(x)+v_\ell(y),\qquad v_\ell(x+y)\geq\min\bigl(v_\ell(x),v_\ell(y)\bigr). $$

The first is a restatement of the fact that exponents add. For the second, write $x=\ell^{a}u$ and $y=\ell^{b}w$ with $a\leq b$; then $x+y=\ell^{a}(u+\ell^{b-a}w)$, and the bracket is a rational number with no $\ell$ in its denominator, so its valuation is at least $0$. Note the strengthening we get for free: if $a\lt b$, the bracket is $u$ plus something divisible by $\ell$, hence has valuation exactly $0$, so

$$ v_\ell(x)\neq v_\ell(y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_\ell(x+y)=\min\bigl(v_\ell(x),v_\ell(y)\bigr). $$

Now invert the scale. Define the $\ell$-adic absolute value

$$ |x|_\ell=\ell^{-v_\ell(x)},\qquad |0|_\ell=0. $$

The minus sign in the exponent is the entire idea: a number is $\ell$-adically small when it is divisible by a high power of $\ell$. Thus $|81|_3=1/81$ is tiny, while $|1/81|_3=81$ is large. The two displayed properties become

$$ |xy|_\ell=|x|_\ell\,|y|_\ell,\qquad |x+y|_\ell\leq\max\bigl(|x|_\ell,|y|_\ell\bigr). $$

The second is called the ultrametric inequality, and it is strictly stronger than the triangle inequality it replaces. An absolute value satisfying it is called non-archimedean. It has consequences that are worth absorbing now, because they are why the word “continuous” will later be doing real work:

  • Every point of a ball is its centre. If $|y-x|_\ell\lt r$ then the balls of radius $r$ about $x$ and about $y$ contain exactly the same points.
  • Two balls are therefore either disjoint or nested — never partially overlapping.
  • Every open ball is also closed. The space is totally disconnected: it falls apart into pieces at every scale.

A geometry made of grains rather than of intervals. It is the natural home of congruence conditions, which is exactly what essays 01 and 02 were manipulating by hand.

Completing $\mathbb{Q}$ in the new metric

Setting $d(x,y)=|x-y|_\ell$ makes $\mathbb{Q}$ a metric space, and it is not complete: one can write down Cauchy sequences of rationals with no rational limit, just as $\sqrt2$ is missing from $\mathbb{Q}$ in the ordinary metric. Filling in the gaps gives the field $\mathbb{Q}_\ell$ of $\ell$-adic numbers, in which $|\cdot|_\ell$ extends and every Cauchy sequence converges.

Stated, not proved. The completion exists, is a field, carries a unique extension of $|\cdot|_\ell$, and contains $\mathbb{Q}$ as a dense subfield. The construction is the standard one — equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences — and nothing later in this collection depends on its details, only on the resulting objects.

Inside $\mathbb{Q}_\ell$ sit the elements of non-negative valuation, the $\ell$-adic integers

$$ \mathbb{Z}_\ell=\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_\ell:v_\ell(x)\geq0\}=\{x:|x|_\ell\leq1\}. $$

This is a ring: valuations add on products and do not decrease on sums, so non-negativity is preserved by both operations. Its units are the elements of valuation exactly $0$, since $v_\ell(x)+v_\ell(1/x)=0$ forces both to vanish. Hence every non-unit is divisible by $\ell$, and $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ has exactly one maximal ideal, $\ell\mathbb{Z}_\ell$. A ring this simple — one maximal ideal, every element a unit times a power of $\ell$ — is far easier to work over than $\mathbb{Z}$.

The same ring, built by congruences

There is a second description of $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ that never mentions limits, and it is the one to keep in mind. Consider sequences

$$ a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots,\qquad a_n\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z}, $$

that are compatible: each $a_{n+1}$ reduces to $a_n$ modulo $\ell^{n}$. Such a sequence is a decision, made consistently at every precision, about what a number is modulo every power of $\ell$. These sequences form a ring under componentwise operations, called the inverse limit, and it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$:

$$ \mathbb{Z}_\ell\;\cong\;\varprojlim_n\ \mathbb{Z}/\ell^{n}\mathbb{Z}. $$

Read the two constructions as answering the same question differently. The metric version says an $\ell$-adic integer is a limit; the congruence version says it is a compatible family of congruences. The second is what makes $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ usable in arithmetic, and the shape of the definition — an object assembled from finite pieces at every level — is the same shape essay 03 needs for a profinite group.

The congruence picture gives coordinates. Write each $a_n$ as its representative in $[0,\ell^{n})$. Compatibility means $a_{n+1}=a_n+\ell^{n}b_n$ for a unique digit $b_n\in\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}$, so the sequence is determined by the digits, and conversely any digit string determines a compatible sequence. Therefore

$$ \mathbb{Z}_\ell =\Bigl\{\,b_0+b_1\ell+b_2\ell^2+\cdots\;:\;b_i\in\{0,\dots,\ell-1\}\,\Bigr\}, $$

with each element having exactly one such expansion. These are ordinary base-$\ell$ numerals extended infinitely to the left instead of to the right. The series converges because $|b_i\ell^i|_\ell\leq\ell^{-i}\to0$: in this metric, high powers of $\ell$ are the negligible ones.

Two remarks on what that argument rests on, because the two constructions are not interchangeable for bookkeeping purposes. First, the digit expansion was derived entirely from compatibility — it is a fact about $\varprojlim\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n\mathbb{Z}$ and uses neither completeness nor the isomorphism above. Second, the same is true of the unit criterion: a compatible sequence is invertible exactly when its first term is non-zero in $\mathbb{Z}/\ell$. So a reader who prefers to define $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ as the inverse limit gives up nothing and assumes strictly less; the metric route is kept first because the ultrametric geometry is what makes the word “continuous” meaningful in essay 05. The ledger records the two facts separately for exactly this reason.

Modules, and what “rank two” means

A module over a commutative ring $R$ is an abelian group $M$ with a multiplication $R\times M\to M$ obeying the vector-space axioms. The definition is the vector-space definition with the word “field” weakened to “ring”, and that single weakening is the reason a new word is needed: over a ring, elements can fail to be invertible, so familiar facts stop holding. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, for instance, $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is a module with no basis at all.

$M$ is free of rank $n$ if it has a basis of $n$ elements, that is, if $M\cong R^n$ with every element uniquely a combination $r_1e_1+\cdots+r_ne_n$. For $R=\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ the rank is well defined, because reducing modulo the maximal ideal turns a rank-$n$ free module into an $n$-dimensional vector space over the field $\mathbb{Z}_\ell/\ell\mathbb{Z}_\ell\cong\mathbb{F}_\ell$, and dimension over a field is well defined.

This is the vocabulary the later essays need, and the reason to insist on it is that “two-dimensional” would be the wrong phrase. Essay 09 builds, for every prime $\ell$, an object free of rank $2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ — not a vector space over a field — and essay 10 makes the Galois group act on it. Choosing a basis then turns that action into $2\times2$ matrices with entries in $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$. The group of invertible such matrices is $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$, and invertibility there is a stronger requirement than invertibility over the field $\mathbb{Q}_\ell$:

$$ M\in\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_\ell(\det M)=0. $$

The proof is the adjugate. For $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ we have $M^{-1}=(\det M)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$, and the adjugate on the right always has entries in $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$. So $M^{-1}$ is integral exactly when $1/\det M$ is, which happens exactly when $\det M$ is a unit.

Rung 3 What it buys

Negative and fractional, written as digits

Work in $\mathbb{Z}_3$ and compute the digits of $-1$. We need $a_n\equiv-1$ modulo $3^n$: $a_1=2$, $a_2=8$, $a_3=26$, and in general $a_n=3^n-1$. Differencing gives every digit equal to $2$:

$$ -1=2+2\cdot3+2\cdot3^2+2\cdot3^3+\cdots $$

Which is believable from the other side: the partial sums are $2,8,26,80,\dots$, marching away in the ordinary metric but converging to $-1$ in this one, since $|{-1}-(3^n-1)|_3=|3^n|_3=3^{-n}\to0$.

Now a fraction. Since $3$ is odd, $2$ is a unit in $\mathbb{Z}_3$, so $\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$ are $3$-adic integers even though they are not integers. Solving $2a_n\equiv-1$ modulo $3^n$ gives $a_1=1$, $a_2=4$, $a_3=13$, so

$$ -\tfrac12=1+1\cdot3+1\cdot3^2+\cdots, \qquad \tfrac12=2+1\cdot3+1\cdot3^2+\cdots. $$

The first is the geometric series $\sum_{i\geq0}3^i$, whose value “ought to be” $1/(1-3)=-\tfrac12$ — and in $\mathbb{Q}_3$ it genuinely converges to exactly that, because the ratio $3$ has $|3|_3\lt 1$. A divergent series in one metric is a convergent one in another.

Essay 01's descent step, restated

Here is the payoff for the earlier essays. That recurring step — pairwise coprime factors whose product is a square must each be squares — is a statement about valuations and nothing else. If $xyz=w^2$ then for every prime $\ell$

$$ v_\ell(x)+v_\ell(y)+v_\ell(z)=2v_\ell(w), $$

an even number. Pairwise coprimality says that for each $\ell$ at most one of the three terms on the left is non-zero. A single term equal to an even number is even, so every $v_\ell(x)$ is even, and a positive integer all of whose valuations are even is a square.

The same sentence handles cubes, and any exponent, with $2$ replaced throughout. Essay 01 proved this by hand each time it was needed. What the valuation supplies is not a new theorem but the observation that these were all one theorem, and that the hypothesis doing the work is coprimality separates the primes.

Where each piece is spent

Three separate debts are settled here, and it is worth naming which essay collects each.

  • The valuation $v_\ell$ is the tool of essay 23. The Frey curve's discriminant is a gigantic product, and the only way to determine its reduction type at each bad prime is to compute $v_\ell$ of that discriminant. That computation is elementary, and it is the one place where a reader can personally verify that the hypothesis of Wiles's theorem is met.
  • The ring $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ and free modules of rank $2$ are the target of essays 09 and 10. Rank $2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ is precisely why the representations in this collection are $2\times2$ rather than some other size, and $\mathrm{GL}_2$ appears because a rank-$2$ free module has a basis.
  • The inverse limit is the shape of essay 03's absolute Galois group and of essay 05's continuity condition. Both are objects assembled from compatible finite data, and neither is a mysterious construction once $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ has been built twice.

Essay 08, immediately after the next Part's first two essays, is where $v_\ell$ first gets used in anger: the reduction type of an elliptic curve at a prime is read off from the valuation of a discriminant.

Rung 4 Reading

Absolute values and the valuation, at the source. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.785 Fall 2021 Lecture 1, Absolute values and discrete valuations, is the primary treatment: definition 1.2 gives the absolute-value axioms and the fourth condition defining the non-archimedean case, lemma 1.4 characterises that case by boundedness on sums of ones, and definition 1.7 defines $v_p$ on $\mathbb{Q}$ together with $|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}$. Definition 1.10 gives a valuation as a homomorphism satisfying the minimum inequality — the two properties proved in Rung 2. Theorem 1.8 (Ostrowski) shows the archimedean absolute value and the $|\cdot|_p$ are, up to equivalence, the only ones on $\mathbb{Q}$: the sunglasses of the title come in exactly one family per prime.

The same axioms, recalled where completions begin. That course's Lecture 8, §8.1, restates the axioms and the strengthened inequality alongside the corresponding metric and ultrametric conditions, and collects the ball properties recorded above.

Completions. The same notes' definition 8.1 fixes Cauchy sequences and completeness, and definition 8.3 constructs the completion of a metric space and extends the absolute value to it.

Inverse limits and $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ twice over. Definitions 8.7 through 8.9 of those notes build directed sets, inverse systems and the inverse limit; proposition 8.11 identifies the valuation ring of a completion with an inverse limit of quotients; and example 8.13 states both descriptions of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ — the valuation ring inside $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $\varprojlim\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ — as two definitions of the same ring.

Digit expansions. Definition 8.16 and proposition 8.17 of the same lecture establish that every $p$-adic integer has a unique digit expansion and that every digit string occurs.

Modules. Any standard graduate algebra text develops modules, free modules and rank over a commutative ring; the facts used here are that a free module of finite rank has a well-defined rank and that $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ is a local ring with residue field $\mathbb{F}_\ell$.

Check it yourself. Run checks/p_adic.py. It computes $v_\ell$ and $|\cdot|_\ell$ with exact rational arithmetic, tests additivity and the ultrametric inequality — including the equality case — over several thousand pairs and five primes, recomputes every digit string in the table above from the congruence definition, confirms that the truncated digit strings agree with the number modulo $\ell^n$, and verifies the determinant criterion for $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_3)$ against the adjugate on every $2\times2$ matrix with entries below $9$.
