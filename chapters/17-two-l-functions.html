Part IV · The two worlds are one — Essay 17

Two $L$-functions, one shape

An elliptic curve supplies one arithmetic measurement at every prime. A newform supplies one coefficient at every integer. Euler products show how each local stream assembles into a single complex function — and exactly where modularity is needed to identify them.

This essay proves an Euler-product calculation and imports an analytic theorem Defines: $L(f,s)$ and $L(E,s)$, including every good and bad local factor. Proves: the good- and bad-prime generating functions and their assembly into the newform Euler product from the Hecke relations already registered in essays 14 and 15. Imports: analytic continuation and the weight-two functional equation. Boundary: none of this is required by the FLT contradiction, and continuation for $L(E,s)$ is not claimed before modularity.

Rung 1 Why package the primes?

The model is the identity

$$ \zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{1}{n^s} =\prod_\ell\left(1-\ell^{-s}\right)^{-1}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}(s)\gt1. \tag{1} $$

Expand one factor as a geometric series:

$$ \left(1-\ell^{-s}\right)^{-1} =1+\ell^{-s}+\ell^{-2s}+\cdots. \tag{2} $$

Choosing one term from each prime factor means choosing an exponent $r_\ell\geq0$ for every $\ell$, with all but finitely many zero. Essay 02 explained why the product $n=\prod_\ell\ell^{r_\ell}$ determines those choices uniquely. Thus the expanded product has exactly one term $n^{-s}$ for every positive integer $n$. Unique factorisation is the bookkeeping mechanism behind (1).

Essay 14 found the same two pieces in the Fourier coefficients of a normalized eigenform: coefficients multiply across coprime indices, and a recurrence controls the powers of one prime. That raises a precise question:

If Hecke eigenvalues behave multiplicatively like arithmetic coefficients, do they also form an Euler product?

They do. The proof is local first — one prime at a time — and then global by the same unique factorisation used for $\zeta(s)$.

Rung 2 The newform $L$-function

Let

$$ f(q)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_nq^n \tag{3} $$

be a normalized weight-two newform of exact level $N$, so $a_1=1$. Its $L$-function begins as the Dirichlet series

$$ L(f,s)=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{a_n}{n^s}. \tag{4} $$

For now, (4) is a function on its initial half-plane of convergence. The general estimate quoted with the definition gives a safe region $\operatorname{Re}(s)\gt2$ in weight two. Analytic continuation comes later and is a different theorem.

One good prime

Fix $\ell\nmid N$. Essay 14 proved

$$ a_{\ell^{r+1}} =a_\ell a_{\ell^r}-\ell a_{\ell^{r-1}} \qquad(r\geq1). \tag{5} $$

Set $a_{\ell^0}=a_1=1$ and form the local generating series

$$ F_\ell(T)=\sum_{r\geq0}a_{\ell^r}T^r. \tag{6} $$

Multiplication by $1-a_\ell T+\ell T^2$ gives constant coefficient $1$, coefficient $a_\ell-a_\ell=0$ at $T$, and for every $r\geq2$ the coefficient

$$ a_{\ell^r}-a_\ell a_{\ell^{r-1}}+\ell a_{\ell^{r-2}}=0 \tag{7} $$

by (5). Therefore

$$ \sum_{r\geq0}a_{\ell^r}T^r =\frac{1}{1-a_\ell T+\ell T^2}, \qquad \ell\nmid N. \tag{8} $$

All primes

At a bad prime $\ell\mid N$, the good recurrence (5) does not apply. But essay 15 made $f$ a full-Hecke eigenform, and remark 24.16 of the source registered with essays 14–15 gives the separate bad-prime relation

$$ T_{\ell^r}=T_\ell^r. \tag{9} $$

Normalization identifies Hecke eigenvalues with coefficients, so (9) gives $a_{\ell^r}=a_\ell^r$. Its local series is therefore geometric:

$$ \sum_{r\geq0}a_{\ell^r}T^r =\sum_{r\geq0}(a_\ell T)^r =\frac{1}{1-a_\ell T}, \qquad \ell\mid N. \tag{10} $$

Finally, if $m$ and $n$ are coprime, the Hecke relations give $a_{mn}=a_ma_n$. Unique factorisation turns the independent prime-power choices in (8) and (10) into the corresponding coefficient $a_n$. In the safe initial region $\operatorname{Re}(s)\gt2$, the complete result is

$$ L(f,s) = \prod_{\ell\nmid N} \left(1-a_\ell\ell^{-s}+\ell^{1-2s}\right)^{-1} \prod_{\ell\mid N} \left(1-a_\ell\ell^{-s}\right)^{-1}. \tag{11} $$

The second product uses a different relation. Equation (8) was proved only for $\ell\nmid N$. At $\ell\mid N$, the operator changes, and (10) comes from $T_{\ell^r}=T_\ell^r$ — not from inserting a bad prime into (5). When $a_\ell=0$, its factor is simply $1$. Theorem 24.27 of the source below records the resulting complete product.

The distinction is visible in the polynomial itself. A good prime contributes a quadratic $1-a_\ell T+\ell T^2$; a prime dividing the exact level contributes the linear polynomial $1-a_\ell T$.

Rung 3 The elliptic-curve $L$-function

For an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb Q$, essay 08 attached a number to every prime by reduction type:

$$ a_\ell(E)= \begin{cases} \ell+1-\#E(\mathbb F_\ell),&\text{good reduction},\\ 1,&\text{split multiplicative reduction},\\ -1,&\text{non-split multiplicative reduction},\\ 0,&\text{additive reduction}. \end{cases} \tag{12} $$

Here the Euler product is the definition. Write the reciprocal local factor as

$$ L_\ell(E,s)= \begin{cases} \left(1-a_\ell(E)\ell^{-s}+\ell^{1-2s}\right)^{-1}, &\text{good},\\[2mm] \left(1-a_\ell(E)\ell^{-s}\right)^{-1}, &\text{multiplicative},\\[2mm] 1,&\text{additive}. \end{cases} \tag{13} $$

Then

$$ L(E,s)=\prod_\ell L_\ell(E,s), \qquad \operatorname{Re}(s)\gt\frac32. \tag{14} $$

The conductor $N_E$ records precisely which primes are bad, hence where (13) stops having the good quadratic shape. Its exponent records more: multiplicative reduction contributes exponent one, while additive reduction contributes a larger local penalty. Thus the support of the conductor locates the changed factors, and its exponents remember their severity.

Level $11$: compare polynomials, not point counts

Return to

$$ E:y^2+y=x^3-x^2, \qquad f_{11}(q)=q\prod_{n\geq1}(1-q^n)^2(1-q^{11n})^2. \tag{15} $$

Essay 16 already displayed the point counts, so here we package their outputs. Put $T=\ell^{-s}$. The curve and newform give the same displayed local polynomial:

$\ell$curve/newform local polynomial
$2$$1+2T+2T^2$
$3$$1+T+3T^2$
$5$$1-T+5T^2$
$7$$1+2T+7T^2$
$11$$1-T$, the split-multiplicative factor

At $11$ both sides have $a_{11}=1$. That is why the factor is linear, $1-T$, rather than the good-prime expression $1-T+11T^2$. The missing quadratic term is arithmetic information, not an omission.

Modularity clue 2: the same Euler product. Clue 1 in essay 16 was the common Frobenius polynomial. Packaging those local polynomials produces matching Euler factors, including the changed factor at the bad prime. The displayed finite list is evidence for the pattern, not a proof that two infinite products are equal. Such an equality requires matching every local factor; essay 18 will supply the theorem that does this for an elliptic curve and its newform.

Two further geometric clues are reserved for essay 18: periods and lattices in the torus-to-curve construction, and the parallel roles of the elliptic $j$-invariant and the modular $j$-function. The Hasse bound and the Ramanujan–Petersson bound reinforce the same parallel without adding another numbered clue.

Rung 4 Analytic continuation, and the boundary

For a weight-two newform of exact level $N$, complete the Dirichlet series by setting

$$ \Lambda(f,s) =N^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s). \tag{16} $$

Imported: Hecke's analytic theorem. The function $L(f,s)$ extends holomorphically to all of $\mathbb C$, and for a sign $w_f\in\{+1,-1\}$, $$ \Lambda(f,s)=w_f\Lambda(f,2-s). \tag{17} $$ This is theorem 24.25 in the source below. Neither the coefficient recurrence nor the formal Euler-product calculation proves it.

There is an essential asymmetry. Modular-form theory gives (17) for $L(f,s)$. Starting only from an elliptic curve and the local definition (14), analytic continuation to the whole plane and the expected functional equation were historically the Hasse–Weil conjecture. This essay does not assume them for $L(E,s)$.

Once modularity supplies a newform $f$ whose local factors agree with those of $E$, one has

$$ L(E,s)=L(f,s) \tag{18} $$

first where both Euler products converge. The right side's analytic continuation then provides the continuation of the left side, and its functional equation transfers as well. The logic runs from modularity to analytic continuation of the curve, not the other way around.

This is why the essay is motivational rather than load-bearing for Fermat's Last Theorem. The closing argument never evaluates either function. Essay 18 will replace “the local factors look identical” by the representation-theoretic Modularity Theorem, which is the form the proof chain actually consumes.
