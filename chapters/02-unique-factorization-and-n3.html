Part I · The old method — Essay 02

Unique factorisation, and where it runs out

Enlarge the number system until the equation factors, and the exponent $3$ falls to the same descent. Enlarge it further and something breaks — not a proof, but an assumption nobody had noticed making.

This essay proves and states Proves: Fermat's Last Theorem for $n = 3$, both cases, in the Eisenstein integers; and that unique factorisation genuinely fails in rings of this kind. States: Kummer's criterion for regular primes.

Rung 1 What we already have

From essay 01: infinite descent as a method, the parametrisation of primitive Pythagorean triples, Fermat's Last Theorem for $n = 4$, and the reduction of the general problem to odd prime exponents. Also the one lemma that did all the work — that coprime factors of a square are individually squares — and the warning that it is exactly as strong as unique factorisation and no stronger.

That essay ended by identifying the obstacle. For an odd prime the sum $x^p + y^p$ does not usefully factor over $\mathbb{Z}$. It factors completely one field up:

$$ x^p + y^p \;=\; \prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(x + \zeta_p^{\,j}\, y\right), \qquad \zeta_p = e^{2\pi i / p}. $$

So the move is forced. Work in the ring generated by a root of unity, factor, and run the same descent. This essay does exactly that for $p = 3$, and then examines what happens for larger primes, because the answer is the reason this collection is twenty-five essays and not two.

Rung 2 The construction

The Eisenstein integers

Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, a primitive cube root of unity. It satisfies

$$ \omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0, \qquad \omega^3 = 1, $$

and the ring it generates is $\mathbb{Z}[\omega] = \{\,a + b\omega : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\,\}$, the Eisenstein integers. Because $\omega^2 = -1-\omega$, this set really is closed under multiplication. Geometrically it is the triangular lattice in the complex plane.

Everything we need follows from one function. Define the norm

$$ N(a+b\omega) \;=\; |a+b\omega|^2 \;=\; a^2 - ab + b^2, $$

a non-negative integer, and multiplicative: $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$, because the complex modulus is. Multiplicativity converts questions about divisibility into questions about integers, which is the whole trick.

The units. An element $\alpha$ is invertible exactly when $N(\alpha) = 1$, since $\alpha\beta = 1$ forces $N(\alpha)N(\beta) = 1$ in positive integers. Solving $a^2 - ab + b^2 = 1$ over the integers gives six solutions, namely

$$ \pm 1, \quad \pm\omega, \quad \pm\omega^2. $$

Six units, rather than the two of $\mathbb{Z}$. This matters later: "equal up to a unit" is a weaker statement here, and any descent has to carry the units along.

Unique factorisation. The norm makes $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ a Euclidean domain — given $\alpha$ and $\beta \neq 0$, the lattice is fine enough that some multiple of $\beta$ lies within distance less than $|\beta|$ of $\alpha$, which is division with a genuinely smaller remainder. Euclidean domains have greatest common divisors by the Euclidean algorithm, hence unique factorisation. So in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ the lemma from essay 01 is available: coprime factors of a cube are cubes, up to units.

The prime above 3

One element deserves a name. Put $\lambda = 1 - \omega$. Then $N(\lambda) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$, and since $3$ is prime in $\mathbb{Z}$, $\lambda$ is prime in $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. Squaring, and using $\omega^2 = -1-\omega$,

$$ \lambda^2 = (1-\omega)^2 = 1 - 2\omega + \omega^2 = -3\omega, \qquad\text{so}\qquad 3 = -\omega^2 \lambda^2 . $$

The rational prime $3$ is, up to a unit, the square of a prime here. It ramifies. That single fact organises the whole proof: the exponent $3$ and the prime $3$ interact, and the argument has to be split according to whether $\lambda$ divides the solution.

Two consequences we will use. First, $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]/(\lambda) \cong \mathbb{F}_3$, so every element of $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ is congruent to $0$, $1$ or $-1$ modulo $\lambda$. Second, $\lambda^4 = (-3\omega)^2 = 9\omega^2$, so the ideals $(\lambda^4)$ and $(9)$ coincide — congruence modulo $\lambda^4$ and modulo $9$ are the same condition.

The cube congruence

Here is the lemma that makes the first case collapse.

If $\lambda \nmid \alpha$, then $\alpha^3 \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{\lambda^4}$.

Sketch: by the first consequence above, $\alpha \equiv \pm 1 \pmod \lambda$. Replacing $\alpha$ by $-\alpha$ if needed — which only changes the sign of $\alpha^3$ — assume $\alpha = 1 + \lambda\mu$. Expanding $\alpha^3$ and collecting terms, the cubic factors as

$$ \alpha^3 - 1 = (\alpha-1)(\alpha-\omega)(\alpha-\omega^2), $$

and each of the three factors is divisible by $\lambda$ — the first by construction, and the others because $\alpha - \omega = (\alpha - 1) + \lambda$ and $\alpha - \omega^2 = (\alpha-1) + \lambda(1+\omega)$. That gives $\lambda^3$ immediately, and a fourth factor follows explicitly: after removing one $\lambda$ from each factor, the three cofactors are $\mu$, $\mu+1$ and $\mu+1+\omega$, whose residues modulo $\lambda$ are $t,t+1,t+2$ in $\mathbb F_3$. One is therefore divisible by $\lambda$. Hence $\lambda^4\mid\alpha^3-1$.

The first case, in four lines

Write the equation symmetrically. Since we may move a term across and absorb a sign, $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$ becomes

$$ x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 0 . $$

The first case is the one where $\lambda$ divides none of $x, y, z$. Suppose such a solution existed. By the congruence above, each cube is $\pm 1$ modulo $\lambda^4$, so

$$ 0 \;=\; x^3+y^3+z^3 \;\equiv\; (\pm 1) + (\pm 1) + (\pm 1) \pmod{\lambda^4}, $$

and the right-hand side is one of $3, 1, -1, -3$. But $\lambda^4$ generates the same ideal as $9$, while $3 = -\omega^2\lambda^2$ has only two factors of $\lambda$. So none of $\pm 1, \pm 3$ is divisible by $\lambda^4$, and the congruence is impossible. $\blacksquare$

That is a complete proof of the first case, and it is worth pausing on how cheap it was. The entire cost was buying the right ring. Once the equation lives somewhere that $3$ ramifies and cubes are rigid modulo $\lambda^4$, the obstruction is a statement about the three numbers $\pm 1$.

The second case: set up the descent

The remaining case is the one in which $3$ divides one of the three rational integers. Choose a positive primitive solution $X^3+Y^3=Z^3$ with $Z$ least. Exactly one of $X,Y,Z$ is divisible by $3$: at least one is, by the first-case argument, while two would force the third and contradict primitivity. Move that one signed cube to the right, make it positive, and call the resulting integers $x,y,z$. Then $0\lt z\leq Z$ and

$$ x^3+y^3=z^3,\qquad 3\mid z,\qquad 3\nmid xy,\qquad \gcd(x,y)=1. $$

The quadratic factor $x^2-xy+y^2$ is positive, so $x+y$ is positive as well.

Because $x+y\equiv0\pmod3$, write $x+y=3A$. The other factor is

$$ x^2-xy+y^2=(x+y)^2-3xy=3(3A^2-xy). $$

The expression in parentheses is not divisible by $3$, since $3\nmid xy$. Thus $v_3(x^2-xy+y^2)=1$. Moreover

$$ \gcd(x+y,x^2-xy+y^2)=3: $$

any common divisor divides $3xy$, while $\gcd(x+y,xy)=1$. Dividing both factors by their common $3$, put $B=(x^2-xy+y^2)/3$. We therefore have coprime integers $A,B$ with $3\nmid B$ and

$$ z^3=(x+y)(x^2-xy+y^2)=9AB. $$

Unique factorisation in $\mathbb Z$ now fixes every exponent. Away from $3$, both $A$ and $B$ are cubes; at $3$, the exponent in $9A$ must be a multiple of $3$. Hence for positive integers $r,s$

$$ \boxed{x+y=9r^3,\qquad x^2-xy+y^2=3s^3,\qquad z=3rs.} $$

The Eisenstein factor, including its unit

Put $\alpha=x+\omega y$. It has exactly one factor of $\lambda$:

$$ \alpha=(x+y)-\lambda y,\qquad v_\lambda(x+y)\geq4,\qquad v_\lambda(\lambda y)=1. $$

Here $v_\lambda(x+y)=2v_3(x+y)$ for rational integers. A prime of $\mathbb Z[\omega]$ other than $\lambda$ cannot divide both $\alpha$ and its conjugate: their difference is $\omega\lambda y$, and then the prime would divide both $x$ and $y$. Nor can it divide both $\alpha$ and $x+y$, because their difference is $-\lambda y$ and the same conclusion follows. This really contradicts integer coprimality: a Bézout identity $ux+vy=1$ in $\mathbb Z$ is the same identity in $\mathbb Z[\omega]$, so no Eisenstein prime divides both $x$ and $y$. Thus the three factors in

$$ z^3=(x+y)(x+\omega y)(x+\omega^2y), $$

every prime away from $\lambda$ occurring in $\alpha$ must consequently occur to an exponent divisible by $3$. Since $\mathbb Z[\omega]$ is a unique-factorisation domain and $v_\lambda(\alpha)=1$, there are a unit $\varepsilon$ and $\gamma=m+n\omega$ such that

$$ x+\omega y=\varepsilon\lambda\gamma^3,\qquad \lambda\nmid\gamma. $$

This is the point at which “up to a unit” has to be resolved, not waved away. Absorb the sign $\pm1$, which is itself a cube, into $\gamma$. It leaves three possibilities $\varepsilon\in\{1,\omega,\omega^2\}$. Expanding once, set

$$ \gamma^3=C+D\omega,\qquad C=m^3+n^3-3mn^2,\qquad D=3mn(m-n). $$

Multiplication by $\lambda=1-\omega$ makes the sum of the two rational coefficients equal to $3D$; multiplication by $\omega\lambda$ makes it $3(C-D)$; and multiplication by $\omega^2\lambda$ makes it $-3C$. Now $\lambda\nmid\gamma$ says $m+n\not\equiv0\pmod3$, while

$$ C\equiv m+n\not\equiv0\pmod3,\qquad D\equiv0\pmod3. $$

The last two coefficient sums are therefore divisible by $3$ but not by $9$. They cannot equal $x+y=9r^3$. Only $\varepsilon=1$ survives, and its coefficient sum gives

$$ 9r^3=3D=9mn(m-n),\qquad\text{so}\qquad r^3=mn(m-n). $$

A smaller solution

If a rational prime divided both $m$ and $n$, its cube would divide both rational coefficients of $\lambda\gamma^3=x+\omega y$, contradicting $\gcd(x,y)=1$. Thus $m$ and $n$ are coprime, and so are the three integers $m$, $n$ and $m-n$. Their product is a cube; each is therefore a signed cube:

$$ m=a^3,\qquad n=b^3,\qquad m-n=c^3. $$

They are all non-zero, because otherwise $r=0$ and then $z=0$. Recombining gives another non-zero Fermat solution

$$ a^3=b^3+c^3. $$

After moving negative terms, this is a solution in positive integers. It is strictly smaller. Taking norms in $x+\omega y=\lambda\gamma^3$ and using the boxed equality above gives

$$ 3s^3=N(x+\omega y)=N(\lambda)N(\gamma)^3=3N(\gamma)^3, $$

so $s=N(\gamma)=m^2-mn+n^2$. This positive quadratic form is at least each of $|m|$, $|n|$ and $|m-n|$: for example $4s=(2m-n)^2+3n^2\geq3m^2$, which proves the claim for $|m|\geq2$, and the cases $|m|\leq1$ are immediate; the other two follow by symmetry. Therefore

$$ |a|^3,\ |b|^3,\ |c|^3\leq s\lt 3rs=z\leq Z. $$

In particular $|a|,|b|,|c|\lt z\leq Z$. The construction has produced another positive primitive solution — the pairwise coprimality above passes to $a,b,c$ — whose right-hand term is smaller than the minimal choice $Z$, a contradiction. The second case is impossible. Together with the congruence argument above, this proves Fermat's Last Theorem for $n=3$. $\blacksquare$

Now do it for every prime — and watch it fail

The obvious plan is to repeat this in $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]$ for each odd prime, factoring $x^p+y^p$ into $p$ linear pieces and descending. For a while it works. Then it stops, and the reason is not that the descent gets harder. It is that $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]$ need not have unique factorisation, and every step that said "coprime factors of a $p$-th power are $p$-th powers" becomes false.

The failure is measured. To each such ring one attaches a finite number $h_p$, its class number, which counts the ideals of the ring up to a natural equivalence. The ring has unique factorisation precisely when $h_p = 1$. For $p \leq 19$ it is $1$. For $p = 23$ it is $3$, and unique factorisation is dead.

Kummer's insight, around the middle of the nineteenth century, was that the descent does not actually need $h_p = 1$. It needs only that $p$ itself does not divide $h_p$ — the obstruction has to be invisible to the exponent. Call $p$ regular when $p \nmid h_p$. Kummer proved Fermat's Last Theorem for every regular prime exponent, which was the deepest result on the problem for over a century.

He also made regularity checkable, by relating $h_p$ to the Bernoulli numbers $B_n$, defined by the expansion

$$ \frac{t}{e^t - 1} \;=\; \sum_{n \geq 0} \frac{B_n}{n!}\, t^n . $$

Kummer's criterion: $p$ is regular if and only if $p$ divides the numerator of none of $B_2, B_4, \dots, B_{p-3}$. This is a finite computation, and it is done in Rung 3.

Rung 3 What it buys

Unique factorisation really does fail — computed

The failure in $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{23}]$ cannot be exhibited by hand. But the phenomenon can, in a smaller ring, and it is worth seeing once so that it is not taken on faith. Work in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$ with norm $N(a+b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$, and consider

$$ 6 \;=\; 2 \cdot 3 \;=\; \left(1+\sqrt{-5}\right)\left(1-\sqrt{-5}\right). $$

The norms check out: $N(2) = 4$, $N(3) = 9$, and $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}) = 1 + 5 = 6$, with $4 \cdot 9 = 36 = 6 \cdot 6$. Now observe that no element has norm $2$ or $3$, since $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ and $a^2+5b^2 = 3$ have no integer solutions — with $b = 0$ we would need $a^2 \in \{2,3\}$, and $|b| \geq 1$ already overshoots. Therefore:

  • $2$ is irreducible: a factorisation would need norms multiplying to $4$, and $2 \cdot 2$ is unavailable, so one factor has norm $1$ and is a unit.
  • $3$ is irreducible: same argument with $9$, since $3 \cdot 3$ is unavailable.
  • $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible: norm $6$ would have to split as $2 \cdot 3$.

The units are only $\pm 1$ (norm $1$ forces $a = \pm 1$, $b = 0$), and $2$ is not $\pm$ either of $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ — the norms already differ. So these are two genuinely different factorisations of $6$ into irreducibles. Unique factorisation is not a law of arithmetic. It is a property some rings have.

Which primes Kummer's method reaches

Applying the criterion is a matter of computing Bernoulli numbers exactly and testing divisibility; the script checks/kummer_regular_primes.py does it. The first irregular prime is

$$ p = 37, \qquad\text{witnessed by}\qquad B_{32} = -\,\frac{7709321041217}{510}, $$

whose numerator is divisible by $37$. The irregular primes then continue $59$, $67$, $101$, $103$, $131$, $149$, $157$, and so on.

Set that against the class numbers and a distinction appears that is easy to blur. Unique factorisation fails first at $p = 23$, but Kummer's method fails first at $p = 37$. The two are different because regularity asks that $p$ not divide $h_p$, not that $h_p$ equal $1$: for $p = 23$ we have $h_{23} = 3$, and $23$ does not divide $3$, so $23$ is regular and Kummer's theorem covers it despite the loss of unique factorisation. That gap between the two failures is the first hint of the strategy that eventually wins — do not repair the broken structure, find the invariant that survives it.

Where this leaves the problem

Kummer's programme is genuinely powerful. Refined and computerised, criteria of this family settled Fermat's Last Theorem for every odd prime exponent below four million. And yet it cannot finish, for a reason that is almost comic: nobody knows whether there are infinitely many regular primes. Heuristics suggest around 61% of primes are regular, and the numerical evidence is overwhelming — but it is not a theorem. Meanwhile it is not hard to show there are infinitely many irregular primes. The method is therefore known to have infinitely many gaps and is not known to have infinitely many successes.

Nor does a second, quite different attack finish the job. Faltings's 1985 proof of the Mordell conjecture implies that for each fixed exponent the equation has at most finitely many solutions, and gives Fermat's Last Theorem for a set of exponents of density one. Density one is not all.

So the classical era ends with the problem morally certain, verified past any reasonable doubt, and unproved — and with the useful lesson that direct assault on the equation is the wrong idea. The next twenty essays do not look at the equation at all. They build two apparently unrelated kinds of object, elliptic curves and modular forms, and the proof arrives as a by-product of the discovery that they are the same thing.

Rung 4 Reading

Euler, and a warning. Euler proved the exponent $3$ in 1753, by what we would now call a $3$-descent. Darmon, Diamond and Taylor's survey Fermat's Last Theorem notes in its introduction that Euler's argument appears to have contained a gap — a useful reminder that descent arguments in rings whose factorisation properties are not yet understood are exactly where errors hide, which is the theme of this essay.

Machine-checked comparison. Mathlib's Mathlib.NumberTheory.FLT.Three and Roelof Oosterhuis's Archive of Formal Proofs development both formalise the full exponent-$3$ theorem by infinite descent. They take different presentations of the descent, which is useful independent confirmation of the completed case rather than a source for the prose above.

An independent UFD descent. Chapter 13 of Ben Green's Oxford algebraic-number-theory notes proves the same exponent-$3$ theorem in $\mathbb Z[\omega]$. Its proof makes the pairwise-coprimality calculation and the unit obstruction explicit, but descends through a strengthened Eisenstein-integer equation rather than through the rational coefficient product used above.

Kummer's criterion. The same survey states the criterion in the form used here: regularity is equivalent to $p$ dividing the numerator of no $B_{2i}$ for $1 \leq i \leq (p-3)/2$, obtained by splitting the class number into two factors and finding a closed form for the one that matters. It also records the 61% heuristic, the open question of infinitely many regular primes, and the verification below four million.

Cyclotomic fields and class numbers. Washington's Introduction to Cyclotomic Fields is the standard reference for class numbers of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$, Bernoulli congruences, and regularity.

Check it yourself. Run checks/kummer_regular_primes.py. It computes Bernoulli numbers from the standard recurrence, derives the irregular primes, and asserts the classical start of that sequence. Then verify by hand that $37$ divides $7709321041217$ — the single number standing between Kummer and Fermat.
