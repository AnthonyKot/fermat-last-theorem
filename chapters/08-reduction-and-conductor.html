Part II · Elliptic curves — Essay 08

Reduction and the conductor

Reduce a curve modulo each prime and count the points that survive. Most primes give a smooth curve and an integer; finitely many give a singular one, and how it breaks is the difference between the hypothesis Wiles could prove and the one he could not.

This essay proves, states, and computes Proves: the trichotomy of reduction types from the repeated root of the reduced cubic, and that the bad primes are exactly the divisors of the minimal discriminant. States: existence of a global minimal model, the Hasse bound, and the conductor exponents — hence that a curve is semistable if and only if its conductor is square-free. Computes: $a_\ell$ and the reduction type for two curves, one semistable and one not.

Rung 1 What we already have

Three earlier essays supply everything used here.

Essay 06 gave the general integral Weierstrass equation, its discriminant $\Delta$ built from the $b$-invariants, and the criterion that $\Delta\neq0$ is exactly smoothness. It also carried the warning this essay finally cashes: the curve $y^2+y=x^3-x^2$ has $\Delta=-11$, while a short integral equation for the same curve has discriminant $6^{12}\cdot(-11)$. Two equations, one curve, twelve spurious factors of $2$ and of $3$.

Essay 07 made the rational points a group, and found on that same curve a point $(0,0)$ of order exactly $5$. That point will leave a visible trace in every count below.

Essay 04 supplies the valuation $v_\ell$, with the two properties used throughout: it adds on products, and it is the exponent of $\ell$ in a factorisation. Every question in this essay is a question about $v_\ell(\Delta)$.

The task is to make “reduce the equation modulo $\ell$” well defined and to extract a number from it. The first half of that is harder than it sounds, and essay 06 is the reason why: reduction is an operation on equations, while we want an invariant of the curve.

Rung 2 The construction

Choosing the equation: minimal models

An elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ has infinitely many integral Weierstrass equations. If one substitutes $x\mapsto u^2x$, $y\mapsto u^3y$ and clears denominators, the discriminant is multiplied by $u^{12}$ — the mechanism behind essay 06's factor of $6^{12}$. Since the discriminant can always be made worse, it makes sense to ask for it to be as good as possible.

A global minimal model for $E$ is an integral Weierstrass equation defining $E$ whose discriminant divides the discriminant of every integral Weierstrass equation for $E$. Its discriminant is written $\Delta_{\min}(E)$ and is an invariant of the curve, not of the equation.

Stated, not proved. Every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ has a global minimal model. The proof is a descent on the discriminant, prime by prime, and needs a criterion for when a model can be improved at a given prime; that machinery is standard and is not developed here. Over general number fields the statement can fail, which is a sign that it is a real theorem rather than a definition.

Essay 06's example already exhibits the notion. The two models had discriminants $-11$ and $6^{12}\cdot(-11)$; the first divides the second, and one can check no integral model does better, so $\Delta_{\min}=-11$. From here on, reduction always means reduction of a minimal model. Skip that clause and every conclusion below becomes a statement about an accident of algebra.

Reduction, and the primes where it goes wrong

Fix a minimal model and a prime $\ell$. Reducing each coefficient modulo $\ell$ gives a cubic equation over the finite field $\mathbb{F}_\ell$, written $\tilde{E}$. Two cases:

  • $\tilde{E}$ is smooth. Then $E$ has good reduction at $\ell$, and $\tilde{E}$ is an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{F}_\ell$ with a group law of its own.
  • $\tilde{E}$ is singular. Then $E$ has bad reduction at $\ell$.

Essay 06's criterion decides which, immediately. Smoothness of $\tilde{E}$ is $\tilde\Delta\neq0$ in $\mathbb{F}_\ell$, and $\tilde\Delta$ is the reduction of $\Delta_{\min}$. So

$$ E\text{ has bad reduction at }\ell \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell\mid\Delta_{\min}(E) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_\ell\bigl(\Delta_{\min}(E)\bigr)\geq1. $$

A non-zero integer has finitely many prime divisors, so bad reduction happens at finitely many primes, and a single valuation computation finds all of them. This is the sentence essay 23 will apply to the Frey curve.

The integer attached to a good prime

At a prime of good reduction, count. The group $\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$ is finite — at most $\ell$ values of $x$, at most two $y$ for each, plus the point at infinity — and one expects roughly $\ell+1$ points, one for each $x$ plus infinity, since about half the relevant values are squares. Define the trace of Frobenius as the deviation:

$$ a_\ell=\ell+1-\#\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell). $$

The name is not explained yet; it is earned in essay 10, where the same integer reappears as the trace of a $2\times2$ matrix. For now it is a count, computable by hand for small $\ell$.

The Hasse bound, stated: $|a_\ell|\leq2\sqrt{\ell}$. So the deviation is small compared with $\ell$, and the sequence $(a_\ell)$ is a genuinely delicate arithmetic fingerprint of the curve rather than a coarse one. Part IV is about the discovery that this fingerprint also belongs to something else entirely.

How a curve goes bad: exactly three ways

Now suppose $\ell$ is bad. The reduced cubic $\tilde{E}$ is singular, and its singularity is highly constrained: a Weierstrass cubic has exactly one singular point, and the point at infinity is never it. Translating that point to the origin and discarding terms of degree three or more, the equation near it becomes a quadratic form in two variables. There are two possibilities for such a form, and they are the classification.

  • Two distinct tangent directions — the quadratic form factors into two different linear forms. The singularity is a node, a genuine self-crossing, and $E$ has multiplicative reduction at $\ell$.
  • One repeated tangent direction — the quadratic form is a perfect square. The singularity is a cusp, and $E$ has additive reduction at $\ell$.

The two names come from the group that survives. Deleting the singular point leaves a set $\tilde{E}^{\mathrm{ns}}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$ of non-singular points which is still closed under the chord-and-tangent rule of essay 07, because a line meeting the cubic at two non-singular points cannot meet it at the singular one — the singular point absorbs at least two intersections, and a cubic has only three. So a group remains, and it is:

  • the multiplicative group of a field, in the node case, whence multiplicative;
  • the additive group of $\mathbb{F}_\ell$, in the cusp case, whence additive.

Counting that group gives the analogue of $a_\ell$. The singular point is rational and has been removed, so the count is shifted by one relative to the good case:

$$ a_\ell=\ell-\#\tilde{E}^{\mathrm{ns}}(\mathbb{F}_\ell). $$

The three possibilities are now forced. In the cusp case the group has $\ell$ elements, so $a_\ell=0$. In the node case it has $\ell-1$ elements when the two tangent slopes lie in $\mathbb{F}_\ell$, and $\ell+1$ when they are conjugate over a quadratic extension. Hence

$$ a_\ell= \begin{cases} \phantom{+}0,&\text{additive (cusp)},\\ +1,&\text{split multiplicative (node, rational tangents)},\\ -1,&\text{non-split multiplicative (node, conjugate tangents)}. \end{cases} $$

So $a_\ell$ is defined at every prime, and at the bad ones it is one of three values that names the reduction type. A single sequence of integers now carries the local behaviour of the curve everywhere.

Semistable, and the conductor

One of the three cases is worse than the others. Additive reduction is unstable: it can be destroyed by passing to a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, whereas good and multiplicative reduction cannot. That asymmetry gets a name.

$$ \boxed{\quad E\text{ is }\textbf{semistable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E\text{ has no additive reduction at any prime.}\quad} $$

Equivalently, every bad prime is a node. This is the hypothesis of the modularity theorem that essay 18 states and that Wiles proved, so the whole Fermat argument depends on being able to check it. The rest of this section is how.

The conductor $N_E$ packages the local information into one positive integer,

$$ N_E=\prod_{\ell}\ell^{f_\ell}, $$

where the exponent $f_\ell$ measures how bad the reduction at $\ell$ is.

Stated, not proved. The exponents are $f_\ell=0$ at good primes, $f_\ell=1$ at primes of multiplicative reduction, and $f_\ell\geq2$ at primes of additive reduction, where $f_\ell=2$ exactly when $\ell\gt 3$. The primes $2$ and $3$ need separate treatment and permit larger exponents. Computing $f_\ell$ in general is done by an algorithm, and we do not develop it.

Only the first of these is load-bearing for Fermat. The Frey curve will turn out to be semistable, so every bad prime is multiplicative and the single fact $f_\ell=1$ at a multiplicative prime is all the closing argument consumes. The additive exponents, the $\ell\gt 3$ threshold and the special treatment of $2$ and $3$ are needed only for the contrast curve below, and the ledger records them as background so that the chain's assumption is not broader than what it spends.

Grant those exponents and the criterion we need drops out, because square-freeness is a statement about exponents and nothing else:

$$ \boxed{\quad E\text{ semistable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N_E\text{ is square-free.}\quad} $$

In one direction, no additive primes means every exponent is $0$ or $1$, so $N_E$ is a product of distinct primes. In the other, an additive prime contributes $f_\ell\geq2$ and makes $N_E$ divisible by $\ell^2$. Moreover, for a semistable curve the bad primes are exactly the divisors of $\Delta_{\min}$ and each contributes exponent one, so

$$ E\text{ semistable}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_E=\prod_{\ell\mid\Delta_{\min}(E)}\ell=\operatorname{rad}\bigl(\Delta_{\min}(E)\bigr). $$

This is the payoff. A hypothesis about behaviour over every finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ has become a computation on one integer: factor $\Delta_{\min}$, check each of its primes is a node, and read off the conductor as the product of the distinct primes. Essay 23 does exactly this for the Frey curve. For an additive curve the identity fails — the conductor is strictly larger than the radical — and the next Rung shows a curve where it does.

Rung 3 What it buys

A semistable curve: conductor 11

Take essay 06's curve, in its minimal model,

$$ E:\quad y^2+y=x^3-x^2,\qquad \Delta_{\min}=-11. $$

Since $-11$ has the single prime factor $11$, the curve has good reduction at every prime but $11$. Counting points at the small good primes gives

$\ell$$\#\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$$a_\ell$$2\sqrt{\ell}$
$2$$5$$-2$$2.83$
$3$$5$$-1$$3.46$
$5$$5$$1$$4.47$
$7$$10$$-2$$5.29$
$13$$10$$4$$7.21$

The Hasse bound holds in each row, with room to spare. And every count is divisible by $5$ — which is essay 07's order-five point making itself felt: reduction at a good prime is injective on torsion of order coprime to $\ell$, so the five-element subgroup found there survives into $\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$ and divides its order. At $\ell=5$ the count is exactly $5$.

Now the bad prime. Over $\mathbb{F}_{11}$ the singular point is found by setting both partial derivatives of $F=y^2+y-x^3+x^2$ to zero:

$$ F_y=2y+1=0\ \Rightarrow\ y=5, \qquad F_x=-3x^2+2x=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\text{ or }x=8. $$

Of these only $(8,5)$ satisfies the equation itself: there $y^2+y=30\equiv8$ and $x^3-x^2=448\equiv8$, while $x=0$ gives $0\neq8$. So $(8,5)$ is the unique singular point. Translate it to the origin by $x=8+X$, $y=5+Y$; over $\mathbb{F}_{11}$ the equation becomes exactly

$$ Y^2=X^2+X^3. $$

The quadratic part is $Y^2-X^2=(Y-X)(Y+X)$: two distinct tangent lines, both with slopes in $\mathbb{F}_{11}$. A node with rational tangents, so the reduction is split multiplicative, and the surviving group should have $11-1=10$ elements. Counting confirms it: the reduced curve has $10$ affine points, one of them the node, plus the point at infinity. Hence

$$ a_{11}=11-10=1, $$

the $+1$ that split multiplicative reduction predicts. Two independent routes — the tangent slopes and the point count — agree.

So $E$ has no additive prime: it is semistable, and

$$ N_E=\operatorname{rad}(-11)=11, $$

which is square-free, as the criterion demands. The number $11$ is worth remembering. It is the conductor of this curve, and in essay 18 it will turn out to be the level of a modular form with the same sequence $-2,-1,1,-2,\dots$ of coefficients.

A curve that is not semistable: conductor 36

For contrast take

$$ E':\quad y^2=x^3+1,\qquad \Delta_{\min}=-432=-2^4\cdot3^3, $$

which is already minimal. Bad at $2$ and $3$ only. Reduce modulo $3$ and the cubic collapses:

$$ x^3+1\equiv(x+1)^3 \pmod 3, $$

a triple root at $x=-1$. A triple root leaves the quadratic part a perfect square, so the singularity is a cusp and the reduction is additive. Counting agrees: $\tilde{E'}(\mathbb{F}_3)$ has four points, one of them singular, so $\#\tilde{E'}^{\mathrm{ns}}=3$ and $a_3=3-3=0$. The same happens at $2$, where $a_2=0$.

So $E'$ is not semistable, and the criterion says its conductor cannot be square-free. It is not: $N_{E'}=36=2^2\cdot3^2$, with exponent $2$ at each bad prime, as the stated rule predicts for additive reduction. Note what fails here —

$$ \operatorname{rad}(\Delta_{\min})=\operatorname{rad}(-432)=6\neq36=N_{E'}. $$

The radical of the discriminant is not the conductor in general. It is the conductor precisely for semistable curves, which is why semistability is the hypothesis that makes the conductor computable by factoring one integer.

What the chain now needs

Essay 09 turns to the torsion subgroup, whose order-five example above was a first glimpse, and essay 10 builds the representation in which $a_\ell$ becomes an actual matrix trace and the good primes become the unramified ones. Essay 23 then computes $v_\ell(\Delta_{\min})$ for the Frey curve at every bad prime, shows each one is a node, and concludes semistability with conductor $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ — the exact hypothesis essay 18's theorem requires. That computation is the one place in the whole Fermat argument where a reader can check Wiles's hypothesis personally, and this essay is what makes it checkable.

Rung 4 Reading

Minimal models. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 24, definition 24.29, defines a global minimal model as an integral Weierstrass equation whose discriminant divides that of every integral model, and the discussion immediately before it explains why a short equation may be bad at $2$ when a general one is not — the point essay 06 made by calculation.

Reduction types. The same lecture's §24.7 works through the reduced singular cubic, distinguishes the double-root and triple-root cases, and derives the three values of $a_\ell$ at bad primes from the order of the group of non-singular points. Its definition 24.28 gives $a_\ell=\ell+1-\#\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$ at good primes.

Semistability and the conductor. Definition 24.30 of that lecture defines a semistable curve as one with no additive reduction at any prime; definition 24.31 gives the conductor of a semistable curve as the product of the primes dividing the minimal discriminant; and the discussion following it records that the conductor is square-free exactly for semistable curves, with the exponent bounds at multiplicative and additive primes and the special treatment of $2$ and $3$.

The two worked curves. In the LMFDB the semistable curve is 11.a3, of conductor $11$; the additive one is 36.a4, of conductor $36=2^2\cdot3^2$ with minimal discriminant $-432$ and conductor exponent $2$ at each bad prime.

Further reference. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves treats minimal models, the classification of reduction types, and Tate's algorithm for the conductor exponent.

Check it yourself. Run checks/reduction_and_conductor.py. With no elliptic-curve library it computes discriminants from the $b$-invariants, confirms that the bad primes are exactly the divisors of $\Delta_{\min}$, reproduces every row of the table above, checks the Hasse bound at all good primes below $200$, verifies that $5$ divides every point count, locates the node at $(8,5)$ and verifies the translated equation $Y^2=X^2+X^3$ exhaustively over $\mathbb{F}_{11}$, and confirms that both bad primes of $y^2=x^3+1$ are additive with $a_\ell=0$.
