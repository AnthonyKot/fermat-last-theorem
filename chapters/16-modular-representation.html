Part IV · The two worlds are one — Essay 16

The representation attached to a form

A newform begins as a holomorphic function. Its Hecke eigenvalues let us isolate a two-dimensional piece of the Tate module of a modular Jacobian, and Galois acts on that piece with exactly those eigenvalues as traces of Frobenius.

This essay exposes a construction and imports its hard theorem Defines: the coefficient field $K_f$, the modular Jacobian $J_0(N)$, the $f$-isotypic Galois representation $\rho_{f,\lambda}$ and its residual semisimplification. Computes: the common Frobenius polynomials of the level-$11$ newform and the conductor-$11$ elliptic curve. Imports: the Eichler–Shimura construction and relation, including two-dimensionality, irreducibility and the good-prime characteristic polynomial. Ledger: Registers L13 as an accepted import.

Rung 1 The two outputs we want to compare

Essay 10 started with an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb Q$ and obtained, for every prime $p$, a representation

$$ \rho_{E,p}:G_{\mathbb Q}\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Q_p). \tag{1} $$

At a good auxiliary prime $\ell\neq p$, it is unramified and satisfies

$$ \det\!\left(XI-\rho_{E,p}(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell)\right) =X^2-a_\ell(E)X+\ell, \qquad a_\ell(E)=\ell+1-\#E(\mathbb F_\ell). \tag{2} $$

Essays 14 and 15 produced a very different object: a normalized weight-$2$ newform

$$ f(q)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_n(f)q^n \in S_2(\Gamma_0(N))^{\mathrm{new}}. \tag{3} $$

The coefficient $a_\ell(f)$ is the eigenvalue of $T_\ell$ when $\ell\nmid N$. The immediate question is whether equation (3), which describes a complex function, can also produce a representation of $G_{\mathbb Q}$.

It can. More strikingly, its good-prime polynomial has the same shape as (2):

$$ X^2-a_\ell(f)X+\ell. \tag{4} $$

The rest of this essay explains where the representation lives, why Hecke operators select a two-dimensional piece, and exactly which step turns (4) from a suggestive pattern into a theorem.

Keep the direction straight. This essay starts with a newform and constructs a Galois representation. It does not start with an arbitrary elliptic curve and prove that a newform exists. That converse direction is the Modularity Theorem, reserved for essay 18.

Two primes with different jobs

The collection fixes the following notation:

  • $p$ is the residual prime — later it is also the exponent in a hypothetical Fermat solution;
  • $\ell$ is an auxiliary prime at which we inspect Frobenius.

Thus every clean Frobenius formula below assumes $\ell\nmid Np$. Some sources reverse these two letters. The mathematics is unchanged, but silently importing their notation would make the exclusions wrong.

Rung 2 How a function enters a Tate module

The coefficient field

The coefficients of a normalized newform need not be rational. They generate a number field

$$ K_f=\mathbb Q\bigl(a_1(f),a_2(f),a_3(f),\ldots\bigr). \tag{5} $$

To make a $p$-adic representation, choose a prime ideal $\lambda\subseteq\mathcal O_{K_f}$ above $p$ and complete:

$$ K_{f,\lambda}. \tag{6} $$

If every coefficient is an integer, as for our level-$11$ example, then $K_f=\mathbb Q$ and (6) is simply $\mathbb Q_p$. Keeping the general notation matters because most newforms do not have rational coefficients.

From the modular curve to its Jacobian

Essay 13 treated $X_0(N)$ as a compact Riemann surface. Its Jacobian is

$$ J_0(N)=\operatorname{Pic}^0(X_0(N)), \tag{7} $$

the group of degree-zero divisor classes on the curve. It is an abelian variety over $\mathbb Q$ of dimension equal to the genus of $X_0(N)$. If that genus is $g$, its $p$-adic Tate module

$$ V_p(J_0(N))=T_p(J_0(N))\otimes_{\mathbb Z_p}\mathbb Q_p \tag{8} $$

has dimension $2g$ and carries a continuous action of $G_{\mathbb Q}$. This is the same construction used for an elliptic curve in essay 10, except that a Jacobian may have much larger dimension.

The second action on the same space

Hecke operators do more than act on holomorphic functions. Each $T_n$ comes from a correspondence between modular curves: one map remembers an elliptic curve with an extra finite subgroup, while the other quotients by that subgroup. Pulling divisor classes one way and pushing them the other makes $T_n$ act on $J_0(N)$ and therefore on (8).

The Galois and Hecke actions commute. So the same large vector space carries two compatible kinds of symmetry:

$G_{\mathbb Q}$how algebraic torsion points move

The newform selects one eigensystem

A normalized newform defines an algebra homomorphism

$$ \theta_f:\mathbb T\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{K_f}, \qquad T_n\longmapsto a_n(f). \tag{9} $$

We use (9) to keep only the part of (8) on which every Hecke operator acts through the eigenvalues of $f$. Schematically,

$$ V_{f,\lambda} = V_p(J_0(N)) \otimes_{\mathbb T\otimes\mathbb Q_p,\ \theta_f} K_{f,\lambda}. \tag{10} $$

The notation suppresses some bookkeeping at old factors and bad primes, but the operation is familiar linear algebra: a commuting algebra acts on a large space, and an eigenvalue system cuts out its corresponding component. Because the Galois action commutes with $\mathbb T$, it survives on that component.

The deep assertion is that (10) is two-dimensional over $K_{f,\lambda}$. Hence it gives

$$ \rho_{f,\lambda}:G_{\mathbb Q} \longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(K_{f,\lambda}). \tag{11} $$

Nothing in the elementary $q$-series calculation of essay 14 proves that dimension. The passage through the geometry and cohomology of the modular curve is essential.

The imported theorem and relation

Imported: Eichler–Shimura in the weight-$2$ representation form. Let $f$ be a normalized weight-$2$ newform of level $N$ with trivial character, let $\lambda\mid p$ in $K_f$, and form (10). Then:
  1. $V_{f,\lambda}$ is two-dimensional and $\rho_{f,\lambda}$ is continuous and irreducible;
  2. it is unramified at every prime $\ell\nmid Np$; and
  3. at every such $\ell$, $$ \det\!\left(XI-\rho_{f,\lambda}(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell)\right) =X^2-a_\ell(f)X+\ell. \tag{12} $$
The construction through $J_0(N)$ and the relation between Frobenius and $T_\ell$ are often grouped under “Eichler–Shimura” in weight $2$; the general $\ell$-adic representation theorem is also associated with Deligne. This collection imports the existence, dimension, irreducibility, unramifiedness and equation (12) together as 16-eichler-shimura.

The load-bearing geometric identity behind (12) is the Eichler–Shimura relation. In the present trivial-character setting, Frobenius on the relevant Tate module obeys

$$ \mathrm{Frob}_\ell^2 -T_\ell\mathrm{Frob}_\ell +\ell=0. \tag{13} $$

Projecting to the $f$-component replaces $T_\ell$ by $a_\ell(f)$, turning (13) into the polynomial (12). Thus

$$ \operatorname{tr}\rho_{f,\lambda}(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell)=a_\ell(f), \qquad \det\rho_{f,\lambda}(\mathrm{Frob}_\ell)=\ell. \tag{14} $$

These equalities are consequences of the imported characteristic polynomial, not a second independent theorem proved here.

Reduction modulo $\lambda$

A continuous representation of the profinite group $G_{\mathbb Q}$ preserves a suitable $\mathcal O_{K_f,\lambda}$-lattice. Reducing that lattice modulo $\lambda$ gives a residual representation over the finite residue field $k_\lambda=\mathcal O_{K_f}/\lambda$. Different stable lattices can give different raw reductions, so the canonical object is the semisimplification

$$ \bar\rho_{f,\lambda}^{\,\mathrm{ss}}: G_{\mathbb Q}\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_2(k_\lambda). \tag{15} $$

At $\ell\nmid Np$, equation (12) reduces coefficient by coefficient:

$$ \det\!\left(XI-\bar\rho_{f,\lambda}^{\,\mathrm{ss}} (\mathrm{Frob}_\ell)\right) \equiv X^2-a_\ell(f)X+\ell\pmod\lambda. \tag{16} $$

The $p$-adic representation in (11) is irreducible; its residual reduction in (15) need not be. That distinction is crucial in essays 24 and 25, where irreducibility modulo $p$ is a separate hypothesis rather than an automatic inheritance.

Rung 3 Level $11$: the bridge in one dimension

The large Jacobian collapses to an elliptic curve

For general $N$, $J_0(N)$ may be high-dimensional. At level $11$, essay 13 computed

$$ \dim S_2(\Gamma_0(11))=g(X_0(11))=1. \tag{17} $$

Therefore $J_0(11)$ is itself an elliptic curve. The unique normalized newform is

$$ f_{11}(q) =\bigl(\eta(z)\eta(11z)\bigr)^2 =q-2q^2-q^3+2q^4+q^5+2q^6-2q^7+\cdots. \tag{18} $$

Its coefficients are integers, so $K_{f_{11}}=\mathbb Q$. The general construction (10) has no extra eigenspaces to remove: $J_0(11)$ is the specific $\Gamma_0(11)$-optimal elliptic curve, and its whole rational $p$-adic Tate module is the $f_{11}$-component. A choice appears only when we later write down a convenient curve in the same isogeny class.

The integral-coefficient form of Eichler–Shimura, with Carayol's conductor refinement, says that a weight-$2$ newform of level $N$ with integral coefficients gives an elliptic curve of conductor $N$, determined up to isogeny. At $N=11$ this is the isogeny class 11.a. The familiar model used earlier in the collection,

$$ E:\quad y^2+y=x^3-x^2, \tag{19} $$

is the member 11.a3; the $\Gamma_0(11)$-optimal representative is 11.a2. This distinction changes the chosen equation, not the good-prime Frobenius polynomials shared by the isogeny class.

For example, at $\ell=5$ both constructions give

$$ X^2-X+5, \tag{20} $$

while at $\ell=7$ both give

$$ X^2+2X+7. \tag{21} $$

One side obtained the middle coefficient by counting solutions to a cubic over a finite field. The other extracted it from an infinite product of holomorphic functions. The Eichler–Shimura relation says that they are readings of the same Frobenius action.

Modularity clue 1: the same Frobenius polynomial. The curve and the newform independently produce $X^2-a_\ell X+\ell$ at every good prime, and in the level-$11$ example their middle coefficients agree. This clue is already a theorem in the newform-to-curve direction. It does not yet say that every elliptic curve occurs.

Rung 4 What it buys, and where it stops

Both worlds now emit the same kind of object

Before this essay, our two routes ended in different categories:

$$ E \longmapsto \rho_{E,p}, \qquad f \longmapsto (a_1(f),a_2(f),a_3(f),\ldots). $$

Now they end in the same category:

$$ E\longmapsto\rho_{E,p}, \qquad f\longmapsto\rho_{f,\lambda}. \tag{22} $$

At almost every prime, both representations are identified by the same trace and determinant data. Essay 05 imported the theorem that traces of Frobenius at almost all primes determine a semisimple representation. Consequently, equality of the good coefficients is not merely a long numerical coincidence: it is enough to compare the two semisimple Galois representations.

The level is already visible

The representation $\rho_{f,\lambda}$ is unramified away from $Np$. Thus the exact newform level from essay 15 already controls where the representation can ramify. This is the first structural hint that a curve's conductor and a newform's level should be paired. Essay 17 will package the same local polynomials into Euler products; essay 18 will state the theorem that makes conductor equal level for the form attached to a curve.

What remains imported

The diagram “modular curve → Jacobian → Tate module → eigenspace” explains the architecture, but it is not a proof of the construction theorem. In particular, this essay has not proved:

  • that the Hecke-isotypic quotient in (10) is exactly two-dimensional;
  • the Eichler–Shimura relation (13);
  • the irreducibility or precise ramification of $\rho_{f,\lambda}$;
  • independence of the residual semisimplification from the chosen stable lattice; or
  • the Modularity Theorem's converse direction.

Those first four items are contained in the registered import on this page. The last remains the named assumption of essay 18.

Reading

The construction used here. CJ Dowd's Berkeley notes Constructing 2-dimensional Galois representations associated to modular forms of weight 2, theorem 1.2 and §§1.3–1.4, construct the two-dimensional representation from the Tate module of a modular Jacobian and its Hecke action. Theorems 2.1–2.2 give the Eichler–Shimura relation and the polynomial $X^2-a_\ell X+\varepsilon(\ell)\ell$; our $\Gamma_0(N)$ forms have trivial character $\varepsilon=1$.

The integral-coefficient special case. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Lecture 24, theorem 24.37, states that a weight-$2$ newform of level $N$ with integral coefficients yields an elliptic curve of conductor $N$, determined up to isogeny. The notes distinguish the original Eichler–Shimura construction from Carayol's later conductor refinement.

Original provenance. Pierre Deligne's “Formes modulaires et représentations $\ell$-adiques”, Séminaire Bourbaki 355 (1968/69), develops the $\ell$-adic representation construction and begins with the model identity $\det(1-\mathrm{Frob}_\ell X)=1-\tau(\ell)X+\ell^{11}X^2$ for $\Delta$.

It checks the displayed example, not the imported theorem.
