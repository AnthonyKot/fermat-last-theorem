Part II · Elliptic curves — Essay 06

Elliptic curves, written correctly

An elliptic curve is not just an equation shaped like $y^2=x^3+Ax+B$. It is a smooth projective curve with a chosen rational point — and the longer equation that definition permits is what keeps the arithmetic at $2$ and $3$ visible.

This essay defines, states, and proves an example Defines: the general Weierstrass model, its point at infinity, and its discriminant. States: that non-zero discriminant is exactly the smoothness condition. Proves in the worked example: a general integral model and a short integral model describe the same curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ while their discriminants differ by $6^{12}$.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essays 01 and 02 treated equations by factoring polynomials and controlling their integer solutions. This Part changes the question. Instead of trying to solve one equation directly, we study the entire set of its rational solutions as a geometric object.

Essays 03 and 05 supply the Galois language that Part II will eventually use, and essay 04 the valuation $v_\ell$, but this first construction needs neither. We need only rational numbers, polynomial equations, partial derivatives, and the willingness to add one point that an affine graph leaves out.

That last move is the unfamiliar one. A point in the projective plane has coordinates $[X:Y:Z]$, where multiplying all three entries by the same non-zero number does not change the point. Ordinary affine coordinates are the patch $Z\neq0$: scale to $Z=1$ and read $x=X/Z$, $y=Y/Z$. Points with $Z=0$ are the points at infinity. This compactification is not ornament. It supplies the identity element for the group law in essay 07.

Rung 2 The construction

The definition, then the equation

Formally, an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ is a smooth projective curve of genus one together with a chosen rational point. “Genus one” names the simplest kind of closed curve after genus zero. Its general theory is outside this collection, and no later deduction in the Fermat chain needs it beyond the theorem stated here: every such curve can be written as a Weierstrass equation, and every smooth Weierstrass cubic has genus one.

The general affine equation is

$$ E:\quad y^2+a_1xy+a_3y =x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6,\qquad a_i\in\mathbb{Q}. $$

A suitable rational scaling of $x$ and $y$ gives an equation with integer coefficients. Homogenising — inserting enough powers of $Z$ to make every term degree three — gives the projective cubic

$$ Y^2Z+a_1XYZ+a_3YZ^2 =X^3+a_2X^2Z+a_4XZ^2+a_6Z^3. $$

Set $Z=0$. The equation becomes $X^3=0$, so $X=0$; projective coordinates forbid $Y=0$ as well. There is therefore exactly one point at infinity:

$$ O=[0:1:0]. $$

It is rational, built into every Weierstrass model, and chosen as the distinguished point. Thus the phrase “a cubic with a rational point” hides an order of ideas: a general genus-one curve needs a rational point before it can be put in this form; once the Weierstrass form is written, $O$ is already there.

Smooth means no pinches or crossings

Let the homogeneous equation be $F(X,Y,Z)=0$. A point is singular if all three partial derivatives $F_X,F_Y,F_Z$ vanish there; the curve is smooth if no such point exists, even after allowing algebraic coordinates. The condition excludes a crossing, cusp, or isolated pinch. It is also what makes the addition rule of essay 07 work everywhere.

For example, the cubic

$$ y^2=x^3+x^2 $$

passes through $(0,0)$. For $F=Y^2Z-X^3-X^2Z$, all of $F_X$, $F_Y$ and $F_Z$ vanish at $[0:0:1]$. This is a singular cubic, not an elliptic curve. A picture would show two branches meeting there, but the derivative test is exact and needs no picture.

The discriminant packages the derivative test

Checking three partial derivatives afresh for every equation would be possible but clumsy. The coefficients can be combined into four intermediate quantities:

$$ \begin{aligned} b_2&=a_1^2+4a_2,\\ b_4&=2a_4+a_1a_3,\\ b_6&=a_3^2+4a_6,\\ b_8&=a_1^2a_6+4a_2a_6-a_1a_3a_4+a_2a_3^2-a_4^2. \end{aligned} $$

The discriminant of the model is

$$ \Delta=-b_2^2b_8-8b_4^3-27b_6^2+9b_2b_4b_6. $$

The basic criterion, stated here without deriving the elimination behind the formula, is

$$ \boxed{\quad E\text{ is smooth}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Delta\neq0.\quad} $$

For the singular example above, $a_2=1$ and every other $a_i$ is zero. Hence $b_2=4$ while $b_4=b_6=b_8=0$, and $\Delta=0$, exactly as the partial derivatives found.

Short form is valid over $\mathbb{Q}$ — and still not enough

When a field has characteristic other than $2$ and $3$, completing the square and translating $x$ remove the $xy$, $y$ and $x^2$ terms. In particular, over $\mathbb{Q}$ every elliptic curve can be written

$$ y^2=x^3+Ax+B, $$

with

$$ \Delta=-16(4A^3+27B^2). $$

So the short form does not fail over $\mathbb{Q}$. The problem is arithmetic rather than geometric: completing the square divides by $2$, removing the quadratic term divides by $3$, and clearing the resulting denominators can insert large powers of $2$ and $3$ into the discriminant. A short integral equation is automatically singular modulo $2$, because its displayed discriminant is divisible by $16$, even when the curve itself has a general integral model that remains smooth there. To study reduction prime by prime, the model matters.

Rung 3 What it buys

A curve that remembers the prime $2$

Consider the general integral model

$$ E:\quad y^2+y=x^3-x^2. $$

Here $a_1=0$, $a_2=-1$, $a_3=1$, $a_4=a_6=0$, so

$$ b_2=-4,\qquad b_4=0,\qquad b_6=1,\qquad b_8=-1, $$

and therefore

$$ \Delta=-(-4)^2(-1)-27(1)^2=16-27=-11. $$

The discriminant is non-zero, so this is an elliptic curve. It is also not divisible by $2$ or $3$, a fact whose reduction-theoretic meaning essay 08 makes precise: this curve is semistable, of conductor $11$.

This curve will return. In the LMFDB it is 11.a3, in the conductor-$11$ isogeny class 11.a attached to the newform 11.2.a.a; that class reappears in essays 18 and 25. The labels are only a bookmark for now — isogenies and newforms are built later — and one distinction is worth preserving: 11.a2, not 11.a3, is the $\Gamma_0(11)$-optimal curve.

Forcing the same curve into short integral form

Complete the square, translate $x$, and clear denominators. One convenient change of coordinates is

$$ X=36x-12,\qquad Y=216y+108. $$

Direct expansion gives the polynomial identity

$$ Y^2-\bigl(X^3-432X+8208\bigr) =46656\bigl(y^2+y-x^3+x^2\bigr). $$

Thus every point of the original equation maps to a point of

$$ E':\quad Y^2=X^3-432X+8208, $$

and the inverse rational formulas $x=(X+12)/36$, $y=(Y-108)/216$ recover it. The equations describe the same elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$.

But the short model's discriminant is

$$ -16\left(4(-432)^3+27(8208)^2\right) =-23944605696 =6^{12}(-11). $$

The curve has not become more singular. The chosen integral equation has become worse by a twelfth power, carrying twelve artificial factors of both $2$ and $3$. This is why an elliptic curve and a particular equation for it must not be confused.

The chain this unlocks

Essay 07 puts an abelian group law on the rational points, using $O$ as zero. Essay 08 reduces an integral model modulo each prime, defines what it means for such a model to be minimal, and asks whether the reduced cubic stays smooth; its answer is organised by the discriminant and compressed into the conductor. Essay 22 then constructs the Frey curve in general Weierstrass form, and essay 23 must choose its model at $2$ with exactly the care exhibited above.

The practical rule is now available: short form is excellent for geometry over $\mathbb{Q}$; general integral form is indispensable for arithmetic at $2$ and $3$. Later essays may simplify an equation, but they must record what the change does to its discriminant.

Rung 4 Reading

Definition and smoothness. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 1 notes, definition 1.1 and definition 1.5, give the formal definition of an elliptic curve and the partial-derivative test for a smooth projective plane curve. Example 1.12 treats a smooth short Weierstrass cubic as a genus-one curve.

The point at infinity. The same Fall 2023 course's Lecture 2, §2.1, writes the general Weierstrass equation, explains its short form when the characteristic is not $2$ or $3$, and verifies that $O=[0:1:0]$ is its unique point at infinity.

Why the general integral form matters. Its Lecture 24, definition 24.29 and the discussion immediately before it, defines a global minimal model in general Weierstrass form and exhibits a curve whose short equation is bad at $2$ although a general equation is not. That is the arithmetic distinction used here and developed in essay 08.

Further reference. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves develops admissible changes of variables, the $b$-invariants, discriminants and minimal Weierstrass models.

Check it yourself. Run checks/weierstrass_models.py. It computes both discriminants from their coefficient formulas and verifies the coordinate-change identity coefficient by coefficient. Then repeat the four-line $b$-invariant calculation for $y^2+y=x^3-x^2$ by hand; the cancellation $16-27=-11$ is the example's whole point.
