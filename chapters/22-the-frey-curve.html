Part VI · The contradiction — Essay 22

The Frey curve

Assume the theorem is false. The solution you are handed is a triple of integers, which is nothing to work with. Frey's idea was to build a curve out of it — and then let the curve be impossible instead.

This essay proves Given a primitive solution $a^p + b^p = c^p$ with $p \geq 5$ prime, and after a normalisation costing nothing, the curve $E : y^2 = x(x - a^p)(x + b^p)$ is an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ with discriminant $\Delta = 16\,(abc)^{2p}$.

Rung 1 What we already have

From essay 01: any counterexample gives one at exponent $4$ or at an odd prime. Essay 02 rules out the first case for $n=3$ and outlines the second-case descent; until that outline is expanded, excluding $p=3$ remains a declared classical input rather than a proof supplied by this collection. After that exclusion, any counterexample occurs at a prime exponent $p\geq5$. If a solution exists at all, a primitive one does — divide out any common factor, since the equation is homogeneous.

From essay 06: a general Weierstrass equation with non-zero discriminant defines an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$. The same essay distinguishes the discriminant of a displayed integral model from the curve's minimal discriminant, a distinction the arithmetic at $2$ below will need.

That is genuinely all this essay needs. The construction below is elementary: a cubic polynomial, its roots, and its discriminant. What is not elementary is the reason for performing it, and that reason lives in essays not yet written.

Rung 2 The construction

Normalising the solution

Suppose, for contradiction, that $a^p + b^p = c^p$ with $a, b, c$ non-zero integers, $p \geq 5$ prime, and $\gcd(a,b,c) = 1$. Two adjustments cost nothing and buy a great deal.

One term is even. The three cannot all be odd, since then the left side would be even and the right odd. Nor can two be even, because primitivity would then force the third even too. So exactly one of $a, b, c$ is even. Permuting the roles — which the equation tolerates, as $a^p + b^p = c^p$ can be rewritten as $a^p + (-c)^p = (-b)^p$ using that $p$ is odd — we may take $b$ to be the even one, so $a$ and $c$ are odd.

And $a^p \equiv -1 \pmod 4$. Since $a$ is odd, $a^p$ is $1$ or $3$ modulo $4$. If it is $1$, replace the triple $(a,b,c)$ by $(-a,-b,-c)$: because $p$ is odd this is still a solution, and it flips the residue. So we may assume $a^p \equiv -1 \pmod 4$.

One consequence is worth recording now, because it is what the arithmetic at the prime $2$ will later use. Since $b$ is even, $2^p$ divides $b^p$, and $p \geq 5$ gives $2^5 = 32$, so

$$ b^p \equiv 0 \pmod{32}. $$

The condition looks like an extra hypothesis but is free, and it is available only because the exponent is at least $5$ — the first place in the whole argument where $p \geq 5$ does real work rather than merely excluding known cases. Essay 23 will spend it at the prime $2$: it permits an integral change of variables to a minimal model whose discriminant is

$$ \Delta_{\min} \;=\; 2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}. $$

Thus the model displayed below has discriminant $2^4(abc)^{2p}$, while the minimal change of variables removes a factor $2^{12}$. Naming that change now prevents the factor of $16$ below from appearing to change silently in the reduction argument.

The curve

Write $A = a^p$ and $B = b^p$, so that $A + B = c^p$. Frey's construction is to take the three quantities $0$, $A$ and $-B$ as the roots of a cubic, and read the result as a curve:

$$ E \;:\; y^2 \;=\; x\,(x - a^p)\,(x + b^p). $$

Expanded, $E : y^2 = x^3 + (B - A)\,x^2 - AB\,x$, which is a cubic in Weierstrass form with integer coefficients. It defines an elliptic curve provided the cubic has no repeated root — and its three roots $0$, $A$, $-B$ are distinct, since $A$ and $B$ are non-zero and $A \neq -B$ (that would force $c^p = 0$).

Nothing about this is inevitable-looking, so here is what motivates it. The solution supplies three numbers with an additive relation. A cubic supplies three roots whose differences are constrained by its discriminant. Frey's construction matches the two: put the solution's terms in as roots, and the additive relation $A + B = c^p$ becomes a statement about root differences — which is to say, about the discriminant.

The discriminant

For a curve $y^2 = f(x)$ with $f$ monic of degree three and roots $e_1, e_2, e_3$, the discriminant is $16$ times the discriminant of $f$:

$$ \Delta \;=\; 16\,(e_1-e_2)^2 (e_1-e_3)^2 (e_2-e_3)^2. $$

Substituting $e_1 = 0$, $e_2 = A$, $e_3 = -B$, the three differences are

$$ e_1 - e_2 = -A, \qquad e_1 - e_3 = B, \qquad e_2 - e_3 = A + B = c^p, $$

and every one of them is a $p$-th power. Therefore

$$ \Delta \;=\; 16\,A^2 B^2 (A+B)^2 \;=\; 16\,\bigl(a^p b^p c^p\bigr)^{2} \;=\; 16\,(abc)^{2p}. $$

That is the whole point of the construction, and it deserves to be stated in words: the discriminant of the Frey curve is sixteen times a perfect $2p$-th power. Discriminants of elliptic curves are ordinarily unstructured integers. This one has every prime appearing to a multiple of $2p$, with the sole exception of $2$. A curve whose discriminant is that special is under enormous constraint, and the remaining essays of Part VI extract the constraints one at a time until they collide.

Rung 3 What it buys

The construction, watched on a solution that exists

The curve above is built from a solution that — as we intend to prove — does not exist, so it can never be written down. But the construction is pure algebra in $A$ and $B$, and it can be exercised on the one exponent where solutions are plentiful: $p = 1$. Take $3 + 5 = 8$, so $A = 3$, $B = 5$, $c = 8$. Then

$$ E \;:\; y^2 = x(x-3)(x+5) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 15x, $$

a perfectly ordinary elliptic curve, and

$$ \Delta = 16\,(3 \cdot 5 \cdot 8)^2 = 16 \cdot 14400 = 230400 = 2^{10} \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^2 . $$

Two things are visible here that matter later. First, the primes dividing $\Delta$ are exactly $2$ and the primes dividing $abc = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 8$ — the bad behaviour of the curve is confined to the primes appearing in the solution. Second, the odd primes appear to even exponents ($3^2$, $5^2$) while $2$ appears to the tenth. For $p = 1$ the exponent $2p = 2$ is unremarkable; for $p \geq 5$ the same computation forces every odd prime to appear to a multiple of $10$ or more, and it is that rigidity the argument exploits.

The script checks/frey_discriminant.py confirms the identity along two independent routes — once from the root differences, once by expanding the cubic and applying the standard Weierstrass discriminant formula in the $b$-invariants. The two share no algebra, so their agreement is a genuine check rather than a restatement. It verifies several triples with $a + b = c$, and then the identity in $A$ and $B$ for larger exponents.

What the curve is for

It is worth setting out the shape of the remaining argument, since this essay has built the object that carries it. Four properties of $E$ will be established, each in its own essay, and each individually reasonable:

  • $E$ is semistable (essay 23). The discriminant's $p$-th-power structure forces the worst kind of degeneration never to occur, so the conductor is square-free.
  • $E$ is therefore modular (essay 23, by the theorem stated in essay 18). Wiles proved modularity precisely for semistable curves, and $E$ is one.
  • The mod $p$ representation of $E$ has conductor $2$ (essay 24). The curve's own conductor is large — it is the product of the primes dividing $abc$ — but its mod $p$ representation is ramified only at $2$.
  • So a weight-2 newform of level $2$ exists (essay 25, by Ribet's theorem), and none does, because that space has dimension zero.

Every step is a theorem about curves or forms. None mentions Fermat. The equation enters only here, in the construction, and it exits as a contradiction twenty pages later.

A note on who did this

The curve is Gerhard Frey's, from 1986, following earlier work of Yves Hellegouarch on associating curves to Fermat-type solutions. Frey's contribution was not merely the formula but the recognition of what it was for: that a solution to Fermat's equation would produce a curve so lightly ramified that it could not be modular, and hence that the Shimura–Taniyama conjecture implied Fermat's Last Theorem. That implication was made precise and proved by Ribet in 1990, and the conjecture itself by Wiles in 1995. The order matters and is often told backwards: the reduction came first, and it is what made Wiles's target worth attacking.

Rung 4 Reading

The construction and its normalisation. Darmon, Diamond and Taylor's survey Fermat's Last Theorem gives Frey's curve in its introduction in the form used here, together with the normalisations $a^p \equiv -1 \pmod 4$ and $b^p \equiv 0 \pmod{32}$, and credits Hellegouarch for the earlier construction. It states there that the curve is semistable, i.e. of square-free conductor. Its treatment of the Frey curve in §2.2 writes the integral minimal model at $2$ explicitly and gives $\Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}$ — facts essay 23 will derive and use.

Weierstrass forms and discriminants. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves is the standard reference for the $b$-invariants, the discriminant formula, and the classification of reduction types.

Check it yourself. Run checks/frey_discriminant.py. Better, do the $p = 1$ case by hand: expand $x(x-3)(x+5)$, compute $b_2 = 8$, $b_4 = -30$, $b_6 = 0$, $b_8 = -225$, and confirm that $-b_2^2 b_8 - 8b_4^3 - 27b_6^2 + 9b_2b_4b_6 = 230400$. Then check it equals $16(3 \cdot 5 \cdot 8)^2$. The agreement of a formula in the coefficients with a formula in the roots is the entire content of the discriminant's role here.
