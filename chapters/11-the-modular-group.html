Part III · Modular forms — Essay 11

The modular group

Part III opens a second front, and for five essays it will not look like it has anything to do with Fermat. It does: this essay's business is to produce four integers, and the argument ends by evaluating a formula in them.

This essay constructs, computes, and states Constructs: $\mathbb{H}$, the action of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, the fundamental domain, and $\Gamma_0(N)$. Computes: the four counts $\mu$, $\nu_2$, $\nu_3$, $\nu_\infty$, with the index checked against coset counting and the elliptic counts against root counting. States: that $S$ and $T$ generate, and that $X_0(N)$ is a compact Riemann surface.

Rung 1 What we already have

Almost nothing from Parts I and II, and it is better to say so than to manufacture a connection. This essay needs the upper half plane, a group acting on it, and the idea of a quotient. The reader's complex analysis is enough.

What carries over is the target, and it is worth keeping in view through all five essays of this Part. The collection's closing essay ends by evaluating

$$ \dim S_2(\Gamma_0(N))=1+\frac{\mu}{12}-\frac{\nu_2}{4}-\frac{\nu_3}{3}-\frac{\nu_\infty}{2} $$

at $N=2$ and getting zero — essay 25, which a reader arriving in order has not yet met, and which essay 13 supplies the formula for. This essay defines and computes the four numbers in it — and nothing else. That is the whole job, and the formula above is the only reason any of the geometry below matters.

One thread does run from Part II. Essay 06's worked curve $y^2+y=x^3-x^2$ has conductor $11$, computed in essay 08; its point of order five came from essay 07; and essay 10 used that torsion to predict its $a_\ell$ modulo $5$. The level-$11$ group $\Gamma_0(11)$ is computed below, and the number that comes out is the reason all of that lines up.

Which hypothesis this Part is building toward. Essay 25 needs two things the earlier Parts do not supply: (F) the dimension formula, and (M) modularity. Part III settles (F) — essays 11, 12 and 13, the last of which derives it — after which essays 14 to 18 turn to (M). Every essay in this Part will say which of the two it serves.

Rung 2 The construction

The half plane, and matrices acting on it

Let $\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}z\gt 0\}$. A matrix $\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ with $\det\gamma=1$ acts by the fractional linear transformation

$$ \gamma z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}. $$

That this stays inside $\mathbb{H}$ is a one-line computation worth doing, because the determinant is exactly what makes it work:

$$ \operatorname{Im}(\gamma z)=\frac{(ad-bc)\operatorname{Im}z}{|cz+d|^2} =\frac{\operatorname{Im}z}{|cz+d|^2}\gt 0. $$

So $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ — integer matrices of determinant $1$ — acts on $\mathbb{H}$. Note that $-I$ acts trivially, since $(-az-b)/(-cz-d)=(az+b)/(cz+d)$, so what really acts is the quotient $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})/\{\pm I\}$. Every count below is a count in that quotient, and forgetting the $\pm$ is the commonest way to get the numbers wrong.

Two elements do all the work:

$$ S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}:z\mapsto-\frac1z, \qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}:z\mapsto z+1. $$

Stated. $S$ and $T$ generate $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. The proof is a descent on $|c|$ using division with remainder; the script below verifies the conclusion for every matrix with entries at most $3$.

The fundamental domain

A fundamental domain is a region containing one point of each orbit. For $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ take

$$ \mathcal{F}=\Bigl\{z\in\mathbb{H}:|z|\geq1,\ |\operatorname{Re}z|\leq\tfrac12\Bigr\}, $$

and the reason it works is an algorithm rather than a picture. Given $z$, subtract an integer with $T$ until $|\operatorname{Re}z|\leq\frac12$. If now $|z|\geq1$, stop. Otherwise apply $S$: since $|z|\lt 1$, the formula above shows $\operatorname{Im}(-1/z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)/|z|^2> \operatorname{Im}z$, so the imaginary part strictly increases. Repeat. The imaginary part cannot increase forever within a single orbit, so the process halts, and it halts inside $\mathcal{F}$.

That argument is why $\mathcal{F}$ is the standard picture: the two moves are exactly $T$ and $S$, and the region is precisely where neither applies.

Elliptic points: where the quotient is not smooth

Most points of $\mathbb{H}$ are moved by every non-identity element. A few are not, and they are the source of the fractions in the dimension formula. Call $z$ an elliptic point if some $\gamma\neq\pm I$ fixes it.

Two exist, and they are visible in $\mathcal{F}$. First, $S$ fixes $i$, since $-1/i=i$; and $S^2=-I$, so $S$ has order $2$ in $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. Second, $ST=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ fixes $\rho=e^{2\pi i/3}$, and $(ST)^3=-I$, so it has order $3$. These are the only elliptic points up to equivalence, of orders exactly $2$ and $3$ — which is where the $4$ and the $3$ in the denominators come from.

$\Gamma_0(N)$, and the first of the four numbers

Now impose a congruence. For $N\geq1$ define

$$ \Gamma_0(N)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}): c\equiv0\ \ (\mathrm{mod}\ N)\right\}. $$

It is a subgroup because the lower-left entry of a product is $c_1a_2+d_1c_2$, divisible by $N$ when $c_1$ and $c_2$ are. Its index is the first count:

$$ \boxed{\quad\mu(N)=\bigl[\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_0(N)\bigr] =N\prod_{q\mid N}\Bigl(1+\frac1q\Bigr),\quad} $$

the product over the distinct primes dividing $N$. The reason is that reduction modulo $N$ sends $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ onto $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/N)$ and $\Gamma_0(N)$ onto the subgroup with lower-left entry zero, so the index can be counted inside a finite group — which is exactly what the script does, for every $N$ up to $25$, over two million matrices.

Cusps, and the last two numbers

The quotient $\mathbb{H}/\Gamma_0(N)$ is not compact: there is a missing point "at infinity", and more than one of them once $N\gt 1$. Repair it by adjoining the rational boundary,

$$ \mathbb{H}^*=\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}, $$

on which $\Gamma_0(N)$ still acts by the same formula. The orbits of $\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}$ are the cusps, and their number is

$$ \nu_\infty(N)=\sum_{d\mid N}\varphi\bigl(\gcd(d,N/d)\bigr). $$

The elliptic-point counts for $\Gamma_0(N)$ are similar products, and both have an arithmetic description that is more useful than the formula:

countclosed formequivalently
$\nu_2(N)$ $\prod_{q\mid N}\bigl(1+\bigl(\tfrac{-1}{q}\bigr)\bigr)$, and $0$ if $4\mid N$ the number of solutions of $x^2+1\equiv0$
$\nu_3(N)$ $\prod_{q\mid N}\bigl(1+\bigl(\tfrac{-3}{q}\bigr)\bigr)$, and $0$ if $9\mid N$ the number of solutions of $x^2+x+1\equiv0$

The right-hand column is worth having, and not only as a checking device. The elliptic points of order $2$ and $3$ came from $i$ and $\rho$, which are roots of $x^2+1$ and $x^2+x+1$; the congruence condition on $\Gamma_0(N)$ turns "how many such points survive" into "how many roots those polynomials have modulo $N$". The script verifies the two columns agree for every $N\lt 200$, which is a genuinely independent check — one route is a product over primes, the other counts residues.

Stated. $X_0(N)=\mathbb{H}^*/\Gamma_0(N)$ is a compact Riemann surface. Its genus is what essay 13's formula computes, and the four counts above are the only inputs.

Rung 3 What it buys

Level 11, and why the $X_0(11)$ thread closes

Evaluate the four counts at $N=11$. The index is $\mu=11(1+\frac1{11})=12$. For $\nu_2$: $11\equiv3\pmod4$, so $-1$ is not a square modulo $11$ and $x^2+1$ has no roots — $\nu_2=0$. For $\nu_3$: $11\equiv2\pmod3$, so $x^2+x+1$ has no roots either — $\nu_3=0$. And $\nu_\infty=\varphi(\gcd(1,11))+\varphi(\gcd(11,1))=1+1=2$.

$N$$\mu$$\nu_2$$\nu_3$$\nu_\infty$
$11$$12$$0$$0$$2$
$2$$3$$1$$0$$2$

Feed the first row into the formula essay 13 will derive and

$$ 1+\tfrac{12}{12}-0-0-\tfrac22=1. $$

One dimension at level $11$. That is the number the whole $X_0(11)$ thread has been converging on: a one-dimensional space contains, up to scaling, exactly one form — and the LMFDB records exactly one newform of level $11$, labelled 11.2.a.a, whose coefficients are $-2,-1,1,-2,\dots$. Those are the $a_\ell$ that essay 08 obtained by counting points on $y^2+y=x^3-x^2$ by hand. The geometry computed in this essay is what forces there to be room for exactly one such object.

Level 2, which is the ending

The second row is the one the collection is named after. At $N=2$: $\mu=2(1+\frac12)=3$; $x^2+1\equiv0\pmod2$ has the single root $x=1$, so $\nu_2=1$; $x^2+x+1\equiv1\pmod 2$ for both residues, so $\nu_3=0$; and $\nu_\infty=\varphi(\gcd(1,2))+\varphi(\gcd(2,1))=2$. Then

$$ 1+\tfrac{3}{12}-\tfrac14-0-\tfrac22=1+\tfrac14-\tfrac14-1=0. $$

The two quarters cancel and the two halves cancel the one. Nothing is left. Essay 25 does this computation again with the formula in hand; what this essay contributes is that the four inputs are not guesses — $\mu=3$ is a coset count, $\nu_2=1$ is a root count, and both are verified as such.

A check the four counts submit to jointly

There is a reason to trust all four at once, beyond checking each. The same combination is the genus of $X_0(N)$, which is by definition a non-negative integer. So for every $N$ whatever,

$$ 1+\frac{\mu(N)}{12}-\frac{\nu_2(N)}{4}-\frac{\nu_3(N)}{3}-\frac{\nu_\infty(N)}{2} \in\{0,1,2,\dots\}. $$

That is a strong constraint: the terms have denominators $12$, $4$, $3$ and $2$, so a wrong value in any one of them would almost always leave a fraction behind. The script confirms the combination is a non-negative integer for every $N\lt 400$ — four hundred simultaneous constraints on four formulas, passed.

Rung 4 Reading

Modular curves and the counts. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 24, theorem 24.8, gives the dimensions of $M_k(\Gamma)$ and $S_k(\Gamma)$ in terms of the genus of $X(\Gamma)$ together with $\nu_2$, $\nu_3$ and $\nu_\infty$ — defining $\nu_2(\Gamma)$ as the number of $\Gamma$-inequivalent translates of $i$ fixed by some $\gamma\in\Gamma$ other than $\pm I$, and $\nu_3$ likewise in terms of $\rho=e^{2\pi i/3}$, which is the definition used here. It records $\dim S_2(\Gamma)=g(\Gamma)$, and notes the interest in $S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ of dimension $g(\Gamma_0(N))$.

Further reference. Diamond and Shurman's A First Course in Modular Forms develops the action on $\mathbb{H}$, fundamental domains for congruence subgroups, the cusp and elliptic-point counts, and the structure of $X_0(N)$ as a Riemann surface.

Check it yourself. Run checks/modular_group.py. It verifies $\operatorname{Im}(\gamma z)=\operatorname{Im}(z)/|cz+d|^2$ numerically; that $S$ and $T$ reach every matrix with entries at most $3$; that the reduction algorithm lands every sampled point in $\mathcal{F}$; that $i$ and $\rho$ are fixed by elements of order $2$ and $3$ and are the only fixed points among those sampled — asserting that the sweep actually reached both, since a sample missing one would pass the "no others" test for nothing; that the index formula matches coset counting in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/N)$ across two million matrices; that $\nu_2$ and $\nu_3$ match the root counts of $x^2+1$ and $x^2+x+1$ for every $N\lt 200$; and that the genus combination is a non-negative integer for every $N\lt 400$.
