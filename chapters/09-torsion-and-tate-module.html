Part II · Elliptic curves — Essay 09

Division points and the Tate module

Solve $nP=O$. For $n=2$ it is a cubic you can factor; for $n=3$ a quartic; and in general the answer always has the same shape, $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$. That number $2$ is why every representation in this collection is a $2\times2$ matrix.

This essay proves and computes Proves: the $2$-division cubic $4x^3+b_2x^2+2b_4x+b_6$; that the Frey curve has full rational $2$-torsion while the conductor-$11$ curve has none; that $E[3]$ for $y^2=x^3+1$ has nine points of which three are rational; and that $T_\ell(E)$ is free of rank $2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$. Proves conditionally: $E[n]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ in characteristic zero — a complete argument on the torus, resting on essay 07's uniformization and nothing further.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essay 07 made $E(\mathbb{Q})$ an abelian group, so "$nP$" means something and $nP=O$ is a question one can ask. It also found a point $(0,0)$ of order exactly $5$ on $y^2+y=x^3-x^2$, and imported complex uniformization $E(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ — which was flagged there as decoration but is about to do real work.

Essay 04 built $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ twice, once as a valuation ring and once as the inverse limit $\varprojlim\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n\mathbb{Z}$, and defined free modules of rank $n$ together with the criterion for lying in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$. The inverse-limit description is the one this essay needs, and the shape will match exactly.

Essay 08 left an unexplained observation: every point count $\#\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$ of the conductor-$11$ curve was divisible by $5$. That was essay 07's order-five point surviving reduction. By the end of this essay the object it lives in has a name and a structure.

Essay 06 supplies the general Weierstrass form and the $b$-invariants, which is the notation the $2$-torsion computation comes out in.

One convention from essay 04 is in force throughout: $\ell$ is an auxiliary prime, free to vary, while $p$ is reserved for the Fermat exponent. Everything below holds for every $\ell$.

Rung 2 The construction

The $n$-torsion

For $n\geq1$ define

$$ E[n]=\{\,P\in E(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}):nP=O\,\}, $$

the $n$-division points. Two things about that definition deserve notice immediately. It is a subgroup, because $nP=nQ=O$ gives $n(P-Q)=O$. And it is taken over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, not over $\mathbb{Q}$: the coordinates of a torsion point are roots of polynomials with rational coefficients, so they are algebraic numbers, but there is no reason for them to be rational. The gap between $E[n]$ and its rational part $E(\mathbb{Q})[n]$ is not a technicality — it is the entire content of the worked examples below, and in essay 24 it is what decides whether the Fermat argument runs.

$E[2]$ is a cubic

A point has order dividing $2$ exactly when $P=-P$. Essay 07 computed the inverse on a general Weierstrass model as $-(x,y)=(x,-y-a_1x-a_3)$, so $P=-P$ says

$$ 2y+a_1x+a_3=0. $$

Substitute $y=-(a_1x+a_3)/2$ into the curve. The left-hand side is $y(y+a_1x+a_3)$, and $a_1x+a_3=-2y$ makes the bracket $y-2y=-y$, so the whole left side is $-y^2$. Therefore

$$ x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6+y^2=0,\qquad y^2=\tfrac14(a_1x+a_3)^2, $$

and clearing the $4$ gives, in exactly essay 06's $b$-invariants,

$$ \boxed{\quad 4x^3+b_2x^2+2b_4x+b_6=0.\quad} $$

A cubic has three roots, each determining one point, and adding $O$ gives $\#E[2]=4$. The roots are distinct precisely when the curve is smooth, so essay 06's discriminant condition is what stops two of these points colliding. And the group of order four has no element of order $4$ — every non-identity element satisfies $2P=O$ by construction — so

$$ E[2]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2, $$

not $\mathbb{Z}/4$. The first instance of the shape.

$E[3]$ is a quartic

Now $3P=O$, that is $2P=-P$. Writing this out with the duplication formula and clearing denominators produces, for a short model $y^2=x^3+ax+b$, the $3$-division polynomial

$$ \psi_3(x)=3x^4+6ax^2+12bx-a^2. $$

Degree four, so four values of $x$; each gives two points $(x,\pm y)$, since $y=0$ would force $P$ to have order $2$ as well as $3$. That is $8$ points, plus $O$:

$$ \#E[3]=9=3^2. $$

Again a square, and again the group has no element of order $9$, so $E[3]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/3)^2$. Two cases is not a proof, but it is enough to see what to conjecture.

The general shape, from the torus

The pattern does not come from the algebra of division polynomials; it comes from the geometry essay 07 imported. Over $\mathbb{C}$ we have $E(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ for a lattice $\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}\omega_1+\mathbb{Z}\omega_2$, and on a torus the $n$-torsion is visible by inspection: $nz\in\Lambda$ means $z\in\frac1n\Lambda$, so

$$ \bigl(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda\bigr)[n]=\tfrac1n\Lambda/\Lambda =\Bigl\{\tfrac{a\omega_1+b\omega_2}{n}:0\leq a,b\lt n\Bigr\} \cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2. $$

A lattice has two generators, so dividing it by $n$ produces an $n\times n$ grid — the rank $2$ that everything downstream inherits is the rank of a lattice in the plane. Since torsion points have algebraic coordinates, $E[n]\subset E(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})\subset E(\mathbb{C})$, and we may read the structure off the torus:

Proved, conditional on essay 07's uniformization. For an elliptic curve over a field of characteristic zero, $E[n]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ for every $n\geq1$. The argument above is complete given $E(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$, which essay 07 states without proof — so this adds no new assumption to the collection, it spends one already on the books. The ledger records it that way: a proof with a named dependency, not a second import sharing one root. And $n=2$, $n=3$ are verified independently by the polynomials above.

The Tate module

Fix a prime $\ell$ and consider the whole tower $E[\ell],E[\ell^2],E[\ell^3],\dots$. Each sits inside the next, and multiplication by $\ell$ maps each down to the one below:

$$ \cdots\longrightarrow E[\ell^{n+1}]\;\xrightarrow{\;\times\ell\;}\;E[\ell^{n}] \longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow E[\ell]\longrightarrow O. $$

The Tate module is the inverse limit of that system,

$$ T_\ell(E)=\varprojlim_n E[\ell^n], $$

whose elements are compatible sequences $(P_1,P_2,P_3,\dots)$ with $P_n\in E[\ell^n]$ and $\ell P_{n+1}=P_n$. Essay 04 built exactly this kind of object and called the pattern an inverse limit; here it is again, one level up.

Now identify the layers. Using the torus description, $E[\ell^n]=\frac1{\ell^n}\Lambda/\Lambda$, and the basis identification

$$ (a,b)\;\longmapsto\;\frac{a\omega_1+b\omega_2}{\ell^n} $$

sends $(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n)^2$ onto $E[\ell^n]$. Watch what multiplication by $\ell$ becomes in these coordinates. Multiplying $(a\omega_1+b\omega_2)/\ell^{n+1}$ by $\ell$ gives $(a\omega_1+b\omega_2)/\ell^n$, whose coordinates are $(a\bmod\ell^n,\,b\bmod\ell^n)$. So the geometric map "multiply by $\ell$" is the coordinate map "reduce mod $\ell^n$" — which is precisely the transition map in essay 04's construction of $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$.

This is worth pausing on, because the two descriptions are genuinely different maps and only one of them gives the right answer. Multiplication by $\ell$ is not surjective as a self-map of $\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n$ — its image is the multiples of $\ell$. Reduction is surjective, with kernel of order $\ell$. Taking the limit coordinate by coordinate,

$$ T_\ell(E)\;\cong\;\varprojlim_n(\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n)^2 \;=\;\Bigl(\varprojlim_n\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n\Bigr)^{2} \;=\;\mathbb{Z}_\ell^{\,2}. $$

So the Tate module is free of rank $2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ — and essay 04 insisted on that phrasing rather than "two-dimensional" precisely because $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ is a ring and not a field. Choosing a basis now turns any automorphism of $T_\ell(E)$ into a $2\times2$ matrix over $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$, invertible exactly when its determinant is a unit.

Rung 3 What it buys

Two curves, opposite answers

The rational part of $E[2]$ varies wildly between curves, and the two curves this collection already uses sit at opposite extremes.

The conductor-$11$ curve. For $y^2+y=x^3-x^2$ essay 06 computed $b_2=-4$, $b_4=0$, $b_6=1$, so the $2$-division cubic is

$$ 4x^3-4x^2+1=0. $$

By the rational root theorem any rational root is $\pm1,\pm\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac14$, and substituting each gives $1,-7,\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,\tfrac{13}{16},\tfrac{11}{16}$ — never zero. So the cubic is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ and

$$ E(\mathbb{Q})[2]=\{O\}. $$

The curve has four points of order dividing $2$ over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ and exactly one of them is rational. Its rational torsion is instead the order-five subgroup from essay 07, which is what essay 08's point counts were detecting.

The Frey curve. For $y^2=x(x-A)(x+B)$ we have $a_1=a_3=0$, $a_2=B-A$, $a_4=-AB$, so $b_2=4(B-A)$, $b_4=-2AB$, $b_6=0$ and the cubic is

$$ 4x^3+4(B-A)x^2-4ABx=4x(x-A)(x+B). $$

Which is no surprise — it is the original cubic back again, because for a model $y^2=f(x)$ the $2$-torsion is exactly the roots of $f$. But the consequence is the point: those roots are $0$, $A$ and $-B$, all three rational. So

$$ E[2]=\{O,\;(0,0),\;(A,0),\;(-B,0)\}\subset E(\mathbb{Q}), $$

and the Frey curve has full rational $2$-torsion. With $A=3$, $B=32$ the three points are $(0,0)$, $(3,0)$, $(-32,0)$, each of order $2$, and $(0,0)+(3,0)=(-32,0)$ — the sum of any two is the third, confirming $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ rather than $\mathbb{Z}/4$.

Two curves, the same cubic computation, opposite outcomes: none rational, all rational. The second is not an accident of the construction but a consequence of it — Frey built the curve by declaring the solution's terms to be the roots.

$E[3]$ on the curve from essay 08

Take $y^2=x^3+1$, the additive contrast curve of essay 08, where $a=0$ and $b=1$. Then

$$ \psi_3(x)=3x^4+12x=3x\,(x^3+4). $$

The root $x=0$ gives $y^2=1$, hence the two points $(0,\pm1)$. Check the order of $(0,1)$ directly: implicit differentiation gives $2y\,y'=3x^2$, so the tangent at $(0,1)$ is horizontal, $y=1$. Substituting into the curve gives $1=x^3+1$, that is $x^3=0$ — a triple contact. So the tangent meets $E$ only at $(0,1)$, three times over, which says $2(0,1)=-(0,1)=(0,-1)$ and therefore

$$ 3\,(0,1)=O. $$

The remaining factor $x^3+4$ has no rational root, since $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4$ all fail. So of the nine points of $E[3]$, exactly three are rational:

$$ E(\mathbb{Q})[3]=\{O,(0,1),(0,-1)\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/3,\qquad E[3]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/3)^2. $$

A rational subgroup of order $3$ sitting inside a group of order $9$ — one line of a two-dimensional object defined over $\mathbb{Q}$. Essay 10 will call that situation a reducible representation, and it is exactly the configuration the Fermat argument needs to rule out.

What the next essays collect

  • Essay 10 takes the action of field automorphisms on $E[\ell^n]$ — they permute these points, because the group law and the curve are defined over $\mathbb{Q}$ — and reads it through the basis above as a homomorphism into $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$. That the target is $\mathrm{GL}_2$ rather than $\mathrm{GL}_3$ or $\mathrm{GL}_{17}$ is settled here, by the rank of a lattice.
  • Essay 24 collects the full rational $2$-torsion of the Frey curve. A rational subgroup is what makes a representation reducible, and Mazur's isogeny theorem is a statement about which rational subgroups can exist. The $2$-torsion is the extra structure the Frey curve has beyond being semistable, and whether it sharpens Mazur's threshold from $p\gt7$ to $p\geq5$ is one of this collection's open questions. It is not the only one: essay 02 still owes the second-case descent for $n=3$, and the exponents $n=5$ and $n=7$ lie outside the Frey route altogether and are carried as an assumed classical input. About keeps the running count; no essay should claim to.
  • Essay 08's loose end is now tied: the $5$ dividing every point count was the rational part of $E[5]$, injecting into the reduction at each good prime.

Rung 4 Reading

Division polynomials and the structure of $E[n]$. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves develops division polynomials, the group structure of the torsion subgroup, and the Weil pairing.

The Tate module. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 25, §25.3, defines $T_\ell(E)=\varprojlim E[\ell^n]$ as the projective limit of the system whose connecting maps are multiplication by $\ell$, describes its elements as infinite sequences of points with $\ell P_{n+1}=P_n$, and identifies $\mathrm{Aut}(T_\ell(E))\cong\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$ with $\mathbb{Z}_\ell=\varprojlim\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n\mathbb{Z}$ — the same two descriptions of $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$ that essay 04 gave.

Lattices and the torus. The same course's Fall 2023 Lecture 14, definition 14.1, and Lecture 15, theorem 15.1 and corollary 15.12, give $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ and its group isomorphism to an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{C}$ — the import this essay's general structure theorem rests on.

Check it yourself. Run checks/torsion.py. With an exact rational group law for general Weierstrass models it derives the $2$-division cubic, confirms it has no rational root for the conductor-$11$ curve and factors as $4x(x-A)(x+B)$ for six Frey curves, verifies each of the three points has order exactly $2$ and that the sum of any two is the third, computes $\psi_3=3x(x^3+4)$ and the order of $(0,\pm1)$, and checks the coordinate description of the Tate module — including that multiplication by $\ell$ fails to be surjective on $\mathbb{Z}/\ell^n$ while reduction succeeds, which is the step the structure depends on.
