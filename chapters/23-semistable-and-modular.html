Part VI · The contradiction — Essay 23

Semistable, and therefore modular

Wiles's theorem has one hypothesis. This essay checks it, by hand, for the curve built out of a hypothetical solution — and the check is elementary enough that a reader can redo every line of it.

This essay proves, and assumes exactly one thing Proves: an integral model for the Frey curve with $a_1=1$, minimal at $2$, of discriminant $\Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}$; multiplicative reduction at every bad prime; hence semistable, with conductor $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$. Assumes: the Modularity Theorem for semistable curves, from essay 18.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essay 22 assumed a primitive solution $a^p+b^p=c^p$ with $p\geq5$ prime, normalised it so that

$$ a^p\equiv-1 \pmod 4,\qquad b^p\equiv0\pmod{32}, $$

and built the curve $E:y^2=x(x-a^p)(x+b^p)$ with discriminant $\Delta=16(abc)^{2p}$. Writing $A=a^p$, $B=b^p$ and $C=c^p=A+B$, the three roots of the cubic are $0$, $A$ and $-B$, and $a,b,c$ are pairwise coprime.

Essay 08 supplies the machinery. Reduction is an operation on a minimal model; the bad primes are exactly those dividing $\Delta_{\min}$; the reduction type is decided by the tangent directions at the unique singular point of the reduced cubic, a double root giving a node and multiplicative reduction, a triple root a cusp and additive reduction; semistable means no additive prime; and for a semistable curve the conductor is $\operatorname{rad}(\Delta_{\min})$.

Essay 04 supplies $v_\ell$, and every step below is a statement about $v_\ell$ of an explicit integer.

One assumption, declared. The conclusion of this essay is semistable, therefore modular, and the second half is the Modularity Theorem, stated but not proved in essay 18. Everything before that implication is proved here in full. Notice what this means about the shape of the whole argument: the only genuinely deep input is modularity, and the hypothesis it requires is verifiable by the arithmetic below.

Rung 2 The construction

Why the displayed model will not do

Essay 22's equation is integral, but it is not minimal at $2$. Its discriminant carries $16=2^4$ as a visible factor and $B=b^p$ contributes at least $2^{2p}$ more, so by essay 06's warning the model may be worse at $2$ than the curve is. Since $2$ is precisely where the argument is delicate — $b$ is the even one — we must produce a better model before saying anything about reduction.

This is what the second normalisation was for. It has been sitting unspent since essay 22.

A model with $a_1=1$

Substitute

$$ x=4X,\qquad y=8Y+4X. $$

Then $y^2=x(x-A)(x+B)$ becomes, after expanding both sides,

$$ 64Y^2+64XY+16X^2=64X^3+16(B-A)X^2-4ABX, $$

and dividing by $64$ gives

$$ E':\quad Y^2+XY=X^3+\frac{B-A-1}{4}X^2-\frac{AB}{16}X. $$

Both coefficients are integers, and this is exactly where the normalisations are spent. Since $A\equiv-1\pmod4$ we have $-A-1\equiv0\pmod 4$, and $4\mid B$, so $4\mid B-A-1$. And $A$ is odd, so $16\mid AB$ requires $16\mid B$ — which $B\equiv0\pmod{32}$ supplies. Without those congruences this substitution leaves fractions behind and the whole essay stops here.

The change of variables has $u=2$, in the sense that $x=u^2X$ and $y=u^3Y+\cdots$, so by essay 08's scaling rule the discriminant is divided by $u^{12}=2^{12}$:

$$ \Delta(E')=\frac{16(ABC)^2}{2^{12}}=\frac{(ABC)^2}{2^{8}}. $$

It is worth confirming that directly from the $b$-invariants rather than trusting the scaling rule, because the identity that appears is a small pleasure. With $a_1=1$, $a_2=(B-A-1)/4$, $a_4=-AB/16$ and $a_3=a_6=0$,

$$ b_2=B-A,\qquad b_4=-\frac{AB}{8},\qquad b_6=0,\qquad b_8=-\Bigl(\frac{AB}{16}\Bigr)^{2}, $$

so that

$$ \begin{aligned} \Delta &=-b_2^2b_8-8b_4^3 =(B-A)^2\frac{(AB)^2}{256}+\frac{(AB)^3}{64}\\[.4ex] &=\frac{(AB)^2}{256}\Bigl[(B-A)^2+4AB\Bigr] =\frac{(AB)^2(A+B)^2}{256} =\frac{(ABC)^2}{2^8}. \end{aligned} $$

The bracket collapses because $(B-A)^2+4AB=(A+B)^2$. So

$$ \boxed{\quad\Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}.\quad} $$

Calling this $\Delta_{\min}$ requires knowing $E'$ is minimal, and at $2$ it is: the substitution removed twelve factors of $2$, which is the most any change of variables can remove, and one cannot repeat the trick because $a_1=1$ is now odd. At the odd primes the displayed model was already minimal, since $v_\ell(\Delta)=2p\,v_\ell(abc)$ and reducing that by $12$ would require $v_\ell(\Delta)\geq12$ together with a coordinate change that this model does not admit. We record minimality as proved at $2$ and rely on it nowhere else: every conclusion below is about $v_\ell$ of the integer displayed above.

Every odd bad prime is a node

Let $\ell$ be an odd prime with $\ell\mid\Delta_{\min}$, equivalently $\ell\mid abc$. The reduced cubic still has roots $0$, $A$, $-B$, now read in $\mathbb{F}_\ell$. Two of them collide exactly when $\ell$ divides one of

$$ A-0=A,\qquad -B-0=-B,\qquad A-(-B)=C. $$

Here is the step that does all the work. Because $a$, $b$, $c$ are pairwise coprime, $\ell$ divides exactly one of $A$, $B$, $C$. So exactly one collision happens and the remaining root stays distinct:

$\ell\mid A$$0,\;0,\;-B$ with $B\not\equiv0$double

A triple root would need two of the three collisions at once, hence $\ell$ dividing two of $A$, $B$, $C$, which coprimality forbids. By essay 08 a double root is a node, so

the reduction is multiplicative at every odd bad prime.

Note how little was used: not that $A$ and $B$ are $p$-th powers, only that the three numbers are pairwise coprime and sum correctly. The exponent $p$ has not appeared in this paragraph at all, and will not until essay 24 needs $v_\ell(\Delta_{\min})$ to be divisible by $p$.

And so is $2$

The prime $2$ has to be handled on $E'$, which is why the model was built. First, $2$ is genuinely bad:

$$ v_2(\Delta_{\min})=2p\,v_2(b)-8\;\geq\;2p-8\;\geq\;2, $$

using $v_2(b)\geq1$ and $p\geq5$. This is the second place $p\geq5$ does real work, and it is what puts $2$ into the conductor at all — essay 24 needs it there.

Now find the singular point of $E'$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$. With $F=Y^2+XY-X^3-a_2X^2-a_4X$ we have, in characteristic $2$,

$$ F_Y=2Y+X=X,\qquad F_X=Y-3X^2-2a_2X-a_4=Y+X^2+a_4. $$

So $F_Y=0$ forces $X=0$, then $F_X=0$ gives $Y=a_4$, and $F=0$ gives $Y^2=0$, so $Y=0$. The singular point is $(0,0)$, and consistency requires $a_4=AB/16$ to be even — which it is, because $32\mid B$ means $16\mid B$ with a factor of $2$ to spare.

The singular point is already at the origin, so read off the quadratic part of $F$ immediately:

$$ Y^2+XY+a_2X^2. $$

Setting $T=Y/X$, this is $T^2+T+a_2$. In characteristic $2$ its derivative is $1$, never zero, so the polynomial is separable and its two roots are distinct. Two distinct tangent directions: a node, not a cusp. Hence the reduction at $2$ is multiplicative as well, split when $a_2$ is even and non-split when it is odd.

The coefficient $a_1=1$ is what made this work. Had the model at $2$ kept $a_1$ even, the quadratic part would have been $Y^2+a_2X^2$, a perfect square in characteristic $2$, giving a cusp and additive reduction. The entire role of the congruence $b^p\equiv0\pmod{32}$ is to buy an integral model with odd $a_1$.

Semistable, and the conductor

No prime is additive, so by essay 08's definition $E$ is semistable, and its conductor is the radical of the minimal discriminant:

$$ N_E=\operatorname{rad}\bigl(2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}\bigr)=\prod_{\ell\mid abc}\ell =\operatorname{rad}(abc), $$

square-free, and divisible by $2$ because $b$ is even. Then essay 18's theorem applies with nothing left to check:

Assumed, from essay 18. Every semistable elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ is modular. Applied to $E$: there is a weight-2 newform of level $N_E=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ whose Galois representation matches that of $E$.

That level is enormous, unknown, and depends on the solution we are trying to rule out. It is also the reason essay 25 needs level lowering: the useful statement is not this one but the one about the representation, whose conductor essay 24 computes to be $2$.

Rung 3 What it buys

The computation, on a triple you can check

No Fermat solution exists, so no honest worked example can use one. But observe what the argument above actually consumed: $A+B=C$, pairwise coprimality, $A\equiv-1\pmod 4$ and $B\equiv0\pmod{32}$. Any triple with those properties exercises every step. Take

$$ A=3,\qquad B=32,\qquad C=35. $$

The displayed model is $y^2=x(x-3)(x+32)$, with $\Delta=16(3\cdot32\cdot35)^2=180633600$. The substitution $x=4X$, $y=8Y+4X$ gives $a_2=(32-3-1)/4=7$ and $a_4=-96/16=-6$, so

$$ E':\quad Y^2+XY=X^3+7X^2-6X, $$

with

$$ \Delta_{\min}=\frac{(3\cdot32\cdot35)^2}{256}=\frac{11289600}{256}=44100=210^2. $$

Twelve factors of $2$ gone, and $210=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7$. The bad primes are $2$, $3$, $5$ and $7$, and every one is multiplicative:

Each $a_\ell=\pm1$, which by essay 08 is what multiplicative reduction means, and the values were obtained by brute-force point counting over $\mathbb{F}_\ell$ — a completely independent route from the root-collision argument that predicted them. At $2$ the prediction was non-split because $a_2=7$ is odd, and the count agrees. Finally

$$ N_{E'}=\operatorname{rad}(44100)=210=\operatorname{rad}(3\cdot32\cdot35), $$

square-free, as semistability requires.

What each normalisation was actually for

Essay 22 imposed two congruences and could only promise they would be spent later. They have now been spent, and they did different jobs:

  • $A\equiv-1\pmod 4$ makes $a_2=(B-A-1)/4$ an integer.
  • $16\mid B$ makes $a_4=-AB/16$ an integer.
  • The extra factor of $2$ in $32\mid B$ is not needed for integrality at all. It makes $v_2(\Delta_{\min})=2p\,v_2(b)-8$ strictly positive, so that $2$ is a bad prime and appears in the conductor.

The last point is sharp enough to test. With $A=3$ and $B=16$ the model is still integral with $a_1=1$, but $v_2(\Delta_{\min})=2\cdot4-8=0$: the curve has good reduction at $2$ and $2$ does not divide the conductor. For a genuine solution this cannot happen, since $v_2(B)=p\,v_2(b)\geq p\geq5$. So the two normalisations are not bookkeeping — one buys the model, the other buys the prime that the endgame is about.

What essay 24 collects

Two things pass forward. The first is the conclusion: $E$ is semistable and modular of level $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, which is essay 25's input (A). The second is the shape of the discriminant, and this is where the exponent finally matters. At every odd bad prime,

$$ v_\ell(\Delta_{\min})=2p\,v_\ell(abc)\equiv0\pmod p, $$

a multiple of $p$ — whereas at $2$ the subtracted $8$ spoils it. Essay 24 turns exactly that asymmetry into the statement that the representation $\bar\rho_{E,p}$ has conductor $2$: every odd prime drops out of the level because its valuation is divisible by $p$, and $2$ survives because it is not. The curve's conductor is huge; the representation's is $2$; and the distance between them is what Ribet's theorem crosses.

Rung 4 Reading

The minimal model at $2$ and its discriminant. Darmon, Diamond and Taylor's survey Fermat's Last Theorem, §2.2, immediately before theorem 2.15, records that for $A\equiv-1\pmod4$ and $B\equiv0\pmod{16}$ the Frey model has an integral minimal model with $\Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(ABC)^2$, which is the identity derived above.

Semistability and the conductor of the Frey curve. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 25, §25.2, gives the same minimal discriminant for $\ell\gt3$, states the normalisation as $a\equiv3\pmod4$ with $b$ even, and gives the root-collision argument in the form used here: $0$ is always a root modulo $\ell$, while $a$ and $b$ are coprime and so cannot both be divisible by $\ell$, hence $0$ is never a triple root. It concludes that the curve is semistable with square-free conductor $\prod_{\ell\mid abc}\ell$.

Serre's optimal level. The same lecture, §25.4, gives the recipe that makes the forward pointer above precise: for an irreducible mod $\ell$ representation attached to a semistable curve, the optimal level is the product of the primes $p$ with $v_p(\Delta_{\min})\not\equiv0\pmod\ell$. That is the mechanism essay 24 develops.

Further reference. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves treats minimal models and Tate's algorithm, which is the general procedure this essay avoids by exhibiting one model explicitly.

Check it yourself. Run checks/frey_semistable.py. It runs every step above over forty triples satisfying the three properties the argument uses: it confirms the substitution lands on an integral model with $a_1=1$, that its discriminant is exactly $(ABC)^2/2^8$ and that $v_2$ has dropped by twelve, that exactly one of $A$, $B$, $C$ is divisible by each odd bad prime so the reduced cubic has a double and never a triple root, that the singular point at $2$ is $(0,0)$ with two distinct tangent directions, and — by brute-force point counting, independently of all of that — that $a_\ell=\pm1$ at every bad prime and the conductor equals $\operatorname{rad}(ABC)$.
