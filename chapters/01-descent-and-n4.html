Part I · The old method — Essay 01

Descent, and the exponent four

The one case of his theorem Fermat actually proved. It needs no machinery at all, it is completely convincing, and it is the reason the rest of this collection has to exist.

This essay proves There are no positive integers with $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$. Hence none with $x^4 + y^4 = z^4$; and Fermat's Last Theorem reduces to the case of odd prime exponents.

Rung 1 What we already have

Nothing. This is the bottom of the ladder, and deliberately so: the whole argument below uses only the integers, divisibility, and greatest common divisors. No curves, no groups, no fields. A reader who has never met any of the objects that occupy the next twenty-four essays can check every line of this one.

That is worth registering, because it sets up the question the collection answers. Fermat's method settles $n=4$ in a page. It also settles, with more work, a handful of other small exponents. It does not settle the general case, and the reason it does not is structural rather than a matter of effort. Seeing the method succeed is the only way to understand what its failure later costs.

Two conventions. Throughout, a solution means one in positive integers — the cases where some variable is zero are uninteresting and are excluded once and for all. And a solution is primitive when its terms share no common factor.

Rung 2 The construction

Descent

The engine is a single observation about the positive integers: any non-empty set of them has a least element. So if you can show that every solution to some equation manufactures a strictly smaller solution, you have shown there are no solutions at all. There cannot be a smallest member of a set in which every member has a smaller companion, so the set is empty.

This is infinite descent. The name is slightly misleading: no infinite process is ever carried out. One descending step, plus minimality, is the whole argument. What makes it delicate is that the smaller solution has to be produced constructively from the original, and that construction is where the arithmetic lives.

Every primitive Pythagorean triple, described

The descent needs one input: complete control over the solutions of $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$. There are infinitely many, and they can all be written down.

Suppose $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$ with $\gcd(x,y) = 1$. First, $x$ and $y$ cannot both be odd: the square of an odd number is $1$ modulo $4$, so two of them sum to $2$ modulo $4$, and no square is $2$ modulo $4$. They are not both even either, since they are coprime. So exactly one is even; say it is $y$, which makes $x$ and $z$ odd. Now factor:

$$ y^2 \;=\; z^2 - x^2 \;=\; (z-x)(z+x). $$

Because $z$ and $x$ are both odd, $z-x$ and $z+x$ are both even; write $z - x = 2s$ and $z + x = 2t$. Any common divisor of $s$ and $t$ divides both their sum $z$ and their difference $x$, which are coprime, so $\gcd(s,t) = 1$. Substituting, $y^2 = 4st$, so $(y/2)^2 = st$ with $s$ and $t$ coprime. A product of two coprime numbers is a square only if each factor is separately a square — this is the first appearance of unique factorisation in this collection, and it will be the last one that goes through without incident. Write $s = n^2$ and $t = m^2$. Then

$$ x \;=\; m^2 - n^2, \qquad y \;=\; 2mn, \qquad z \;=\; m^2 + n^2, $$

with $m > n > 0$ coprime. They have opposite parity, since $z = m^2+n^2$ is odd. Conversely every such $m, n$ gives a primitive triple. Nothing has been lost: this is all of them.

The theorem

There are no positive integers $x, y, z$ with $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$.

Note that this is stronger than the case $n = 4$ of Fermat's claim, which only forbids $z$ from being a perfect square. Proving the stronger statement is what makes the descent work, because the class of equations is then closed under the construction.

Suppose solutions exist. Among them choose one with $z$ as small as possible. Everything below derives a solution with a strictly smaller third term.

Step 1: the solution is primitive. If a prime $q$ divided both $x$ and $y$, then $q^4$ would divide $z^2$, so $q^2$ divides $z$, and $(x/q,\; y/q,\; z/q^2)$ is a solution with a smaller third term. Minimality forbids this, so $\gcd(x,y) = 1$.

Step 2: read it as a Pythagorean triple. Since $(x^2)^2 + (y^2)^2 = z^2$, the triple $(x^2, y^2, z)$ is primitive Pythagorean. As above, one of $x^2, y^2$ is even; say $y$ is even. The parametrisation gives coprime $m > n > 0$ of opposite parity with

$$ x^2 = m^2 - n^2, \qquad y^2 = 2mn, \qquad z = m^2 + n^2. $$

Step 3: descend once, inside the parametrisation. The first equation says $x^2 + n^2 = m^2$, another primitive triple. Here $x$ is odd, so this time it is $n$ that must be even and $m$ that is odd. Applying the parametrisation again gives coprime $a > b > 0$ of opposite parity with

$$ x = a^2 - b^2, \qquad n = 2ab, \qquad m = a^2 + b^2. $$

Step 4: extract three squares. Substitute into $y^2 = 2mn$:

$$ y^2 \;=\; 2 \cdot (a^2+b^2) \cdot 2ab \;=\; 4ab(a^2+b^2), \qquad \text{so} \qquad \left(\tfrac{y}{2}\right)^{2} = ab\,(a^2+b^2). $$

The three factors $a$, $b$, $a^2+b^2$ are pairwise coprime: $a$ and $b$ are coprime by construction, and any common divisor of $a$ and $a^2+b^2$ divides $b^2$, hence is $1$; likewise for $b$. A product of pairwise coprime numbers is a square only if each factor is. So

$$ a = u^2, \qquad b = v^2, \qquad a^2 + b^2 = w^2 $$

for some positive integers $u, v, w$.

Step 5: the smaller solution. Substituting the first two into the third,

$$ u^4 + v^4 \;=\; a^2 + b^2 \;=\; w^2. $$

That is a solution of the same equation. Is it smaller? Since $w \geq 1$ we have $w \leq w^2 = a^2 + b^2 = m$, and since $m \geq 1$ we have $m \leq m^2$, and since $n > 0$ we have $m^2 < m^2 + n^2 = z$. Chaining these,

$$ w \;\leq\; w^2 \;=\; m \;\leq\; m^2 \;<\; m^2 + n^2 \;=\; z. $$

So $w < z$, contradicting the minimality of $z$. No solution exists. $\blacksquare$

The case $n = 4$

Immediately: if $x^4 + y^4 = z^4$ then $x^4 + y^4 = (z^2)^2$, which the theorem forbids. This is the only case of his Last Theorem for which Fermat left a proof.

Rung 3 What it buys

The exponents collapse to primes

The result above does more than dispose of one exponent; it removes an infinite family of them, and it does so by a triviality worth spelling out. Every integer $n \geq 3$ is divisible either by $4$ or by an odd prime. (If $n$ has no odd prime factor it is a power of two, and being at least $3$ it is then divisible by $4$.)

Now suppose $x^n + y^n = z^n$ with $n = dk$. Then

$$ (x^{k})^{d} + (y^{k})^{d} = (z^{k})^{d}, $$

so a solution for exponent $n$ yields one for every divisor $d$ of $n$. Taking $d = 4$ or $d = p$ an odd prime, we conclude: if Fermat's Last Theorem holds for the exponent $4$ and for every odd prime exponent, it holds for every $n \geq 3$. The first half is now done. What remains, forever after, is the odd primes.

Essay 02 removes $p = 3$ by a similar descent carried out in a larger number system, leaving the primes $p \geq 5$ — which is exactly the hypothesis under which the Frey curve is built in essay 22.

A worked descent, made visible

The proof is a contradiction argument, so it never exhibits anything. But the machinery inside it is constructive and can be watched. Take the primitive triple with $m = 2$, $n = 1$: $x = 3$, $y = 4$, $z = 5$. Step 3 of the proof would next demand that $x^2 + n^2 = m^2$, i.e. $9 + 1 = 4$, which is false — so this triple is simply not of the shape the proof produces, and the descent never starts. That is the normal situation. The proof's content is that the constraints of steps 2 through 4 can never be met simultaneously, and each individual attempt failing early is what that looks like from the outside.

Where the mechanism does bite is in the coprime-factors-are-squares step, and that step is worth testing, because everything later in this collection turns on when it is available. It holds in the ordinary integers. Consider $ab = 36$ with $a = 4$, $b = 9$: coprime, product a square, and indeed each is a square. But drop coprimality — $a = 2$, $b = 18$ — and the conclusion dies while the hypothesis on the product survives. The step is exactly as strong as the uniqueness of prime factorisation, no more.

Why this method cannot finish the job

Look at what made the descent possible. At two separate points the argument factored a difference of squares and then asserted that coprime factors of a square are individually squares. For exponent $4$ these factorisations are available inside $\mathbb{Z}$, because $z^2 - x^2 = (z-x)(z+x)$.

For an odd prime $p$, the analogous move would factor $x^p + y^p$. Over the integers it offers almost nothing. It factors completely only if one is willing to work with $p$-th roots of unity — in the ring generated by $\zeta_p$, where

$$ x^p + y^p \;=\; \prod_{j=0}^{p-1}\left(x + \zeta_p^{\,j}\, y\right). $$

That is the natural continuation, and it is what essay 02 pursues. It works beautifully for $p = 3$. It then fails, and the failure is not a gap in anyone's ingenuity but a genuine arithmetic fact about those rings: they need not have unique factorisation. Every abstraction in Parts I through IV is downstream of that failure.

Rung 4 Reading

The descent and the reduction. Darmon, Diamond and Taylor's expository survey Fermat's Last Theorem opens with this material: Fermat proved the stronger statement about $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$, and the observation that every $n \geq 3$ is divisible by an odd prime or by $4$ is what reduces the problem to prime exponents. That survey also notes something the proof above quietly illustrates — solutions to $x^4+y^4=z^2$ correspond to rational points on an elliptic curve, so even Fermat's elementary argument was, in retrospect, about the objects of Part II.

Pythagorean triples. Any introduction to elementary number theory parametrises them; the derivation above is self-contained and needs no reference. unverified pointers are avoided here because none are needed.

Check the descent yourself. The single step worth doing by hand is Step 4. Take coprime $a, b$ of opposite parity, form $ab(a^2+b^2)$, and confirm the three factors are pairwise coprime. Then ask what would go wrong if $a$ and $b$ shared a factor — that is the crack through which the whole of the next essay enters.
