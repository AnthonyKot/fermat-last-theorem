Part III · Modular forms — Essay 15

Newforms, and the meaning of the level

A modular form can be viewed at every multiple of its original level. Newform theory removes those inherited copies, isolates what is genuinely born at each level, and turns “the level of a form” from a choice of address into an invariant.

This essay computes one obstruction and imports its repair Defines: degeneracy maps, oldspaces, newspaces, bad-prime operators and normalized newforms. Proves: at level $22$, the two copies $f_{11}(z)$ and $f_{11}(2z)$ form one two-dimensional good-Hecke eigenspace; $U_2$ has non-real eigenvalues; and the whole space is old. Imports: the Atkin–Lehner–Li newform theorem, including uniqueness and the full-Hecke eigenform statement. Ledger: Registers L12 as an accepted import.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essay 14 left us with a finite-dimensional space $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ and a commuting family of Hermitian operators

$$ T_n,\qquad \gcd(n,N)=1. $$

The spectral theorem decomposes the space into simultaneous eigenspaces for those good operators. For a normalized vector in such an eigenspace, the eigenvalue of $T_n$ is its Fourier coefficient $a_n$. That was enough to explain multiplicativity, but not enough to produce the objects needed by modularity and level lowering.

The difficulty begins with a simple containment. If $M\mid N$, then

$$ \Gamma_0(N)\subseteq\Gamma_0(M). $$

Consequently a form of level $M$ is automatically a form of level $N$. Merely writing

$$ f\in S_k(\Gamma_0(N)) $$

does not say that $N$ is the first or smallest level at which $f$ occurs. It gives an ambient room, not a birthplace.

There are two linked failures:

  • a lower-level eigenform can enter a higher-level space in several independent ways, and all good Hecke operators give those copies the same eigenvalues;
  • at a prime $p\mid N$, the operator is no longer Essay 14's Hermitian $T_p$.
The boundary from essay 14 remains in force. Nothing below deduces the bad-prime behavior from simultaneous diagonalization. We define the bad-prime operator and compute it directly. The theorem that then organizes all primes is a second, separately named import: Atkin–Lehner–Li theory.

Rung 2 The construction

Two ways to raise the level

Let $M\mid N$ and let $d\mid N/M$. The corresponding degeneracy map sends

$$ V_d:S_k(\Gamma_0(M))\longrightarrow S_k(\Gamma_0(N)), \qquad (V_df)(z)=f(dz). \tag{1} $$

On a $q$-expansion it is almost embarrassingly concrete:

$$ f(q)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_nq^n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_df(q)=f(q^d)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_nq^{dn}. \tag{2} $$

The case $d=1$ simply regards the same function at the larger level. The other divisors create shifted copies. Their span, over every proper divisor $M$ of $N$, is the oldspace:

$$ S_k(\Gamma_0(N))^{\mathrm{old}} = \sum_{\substack{M\mid N\\M\lt N}} \ \sum_{d\mid N/M}V_dS_k(\Gamma_0(M)). \tag{3} $$

The Petersson orthogonal complement is the newspace:

$$ S_k(\Gamma_0(N))^{\mathrm{new}} = \left(S_k(\Gamma_0(N))^{\mathrm{old}}\right)^\perp. \tag{4} $$

“Old” and “new” are not judgments about importance. They record provenance: did this eigensystem already exist at a proper divisor of $N$, or does it first occur here?

The level-$11$ form moves to level $22$

Bring back the form that has followed us through Part III:

$$ f=f_{11}(q) =\bigl(\eta(z)\eta(11z)\bigr)^2 =q-2q^2-q^3+2q^4+q^5+\cdots. \tag{5} $$

Essay 13 computed $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(11))=1$. Raise the level from $11$ to $22$. Since $22/11=2$, equations (1)–(2) give two copies:

$$ f(q)=q-2q^2-q^3+2q^4+\cdots, \qquad V_2f(q)=f(q^2)=q^2-2q^4-q^6+\cdots. \tag{6} $$

They are linearly independent: the first has coefficient $1$ at $q$, while the second has coefficient $0$ there and coefficient $1$ at $q^2$. The same dimension formula gives

$$ \dim S_2(\Gamma_0(22)) =1+\frac{36}{12}-\frac04-\frac03-\frac42 =2. \tag{7} $$

Thus these two vectors are not merely part of the level-$22$ space. They are all of it:

$$ S_2(\Gamma_0(22)) =\operatorname{span}\{f,V_2f\} =S_2(\Gamma_0(22))^{\mathrm{old}}, \qquad S_2(\Gamma_0(22))^{\mathrm{new}}=0. \tag{8} $$

This is the first example where “there are cusp forms at level $N$” and “there is a newform of level $N$” give different answers. The former is true at $22$; the latter is false.

Why the good operators cannot separate the copies

For every $n$ coprime to $N$, the good Hecke operator commutes with each degeneracy map:

$$ T_nV_d=V_dT_n. \tag{9} $$

If $T_nf=a_nf$, then

$$ T_n(V_2f)=V_2(T_nf)=a_nV_2f. \tag{10} $$

So at level $22$, both $f$ and $V_2f$ have the same eigenvalue $a_n$ for every $\gcd(n,22)=1$. The common good-Hecke eigenspace is two-dimensional. This is not a rare technical nuisance; it is the mechanism that creates oldforms. The spectral theorem did its job correctly. The data supplied to it simply cannot distinguish the two copies.

The missing operator at the bad prime

At $p\mid N$, write the operator as $U_p$ to keep its different formula visible:

$$ U_p\left(\sum_{n\geq1}b_nq^n\right) =\sum_{n\geq1}b_{pn}q^n. \tag{11} $$

At level $22$, the new bad prime is $2$. Equation (11) immediately gives

$$ U_2(V_2f)=f. \tag{12} $$

At level $11$, however, $2$ was a good prime. Essay 14's coefficient formula says

$$ T_2f=U_2f+2V_2f=a_2f=-2f, $$

so, now viewing every term at level $22$,

$$ U_2f=-2f-2V_2f. \tag{13} $$

In the ordered basis $(f,V_2f)$, equations (12)–(13) give

$$ [U_2]= \begin{pmatrix} -2&1\\ -2&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det(XI-[U_2])=X^2+2X+2. \tag{14} $$

Its eigenvalues are $-1\pm i$. A Hermitian operator has real eigenvalues, so $U_2$ is not Hermitian for the Petersson product. This one matrix closes the tempting but invalid shortcut: the bad-prime operators do not inherit essay 14's spectral-theorem argument.

The imported repair: Atkin–Lehner–Li

Imported: the newform theorem. For the spaces used here, Atkin–Lehner–Li theory proves that:
  1. the oldspace and newspace are preserved by the full Hecke algebra, including the operators at primes dividing $N$;
  2. a simultaneous good-Hecke eigenvector in the newspace is automatically an eigenvector for every Hecke operator;
  3. after normalizing $a_1=1$, the resulting newforms form a unique orthogonal basis of the newspace; and
  4. every eigensystem at level $N$ comes from a unique normalized newform at a unique divisor $M\mid N$, together with its degeneracy copies $V_df$ for $d\mid N/M$.
This is not proved by the preceding $U_2$ calculation. Its proof controls adjoints at bad primes using Fricke–Atkin–Lehner operators and proves the multiplicity-one statement that good eigenvalues determine a primitive form. Both are substantial inputs, and both are included in the registered import 15-newforms.

A normalized newform — also called a primitive form — is therefore a normalized simultaneous Hecke eigenform in $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))^{\mathrm{new}}$. The adjective “new” supplies the missing minimality, and “normalized” removes the remaining scalar ambiguity.

What “the level” now means

There are now two integers that must not be confused:

integerwhat it saysis it intrinsic?
ambient level $N$ the form is being viewed in $S_k(\Gamma_0(N))$ no; it can be replaced by a multiple
newform level $M$ the unique level where the primitive eigensystem first occurs yes

Our function $f_{11}$ belongs to $S_2(\Gamma_0(22))$, but it is old there. Its exact newform level remains $11$. Conversely, equation (8) says that no primitive eigensystem has exact level $22$.

This is the sense of “level” that later theorems use. When Ribet lowers a residual representation from one modular level to another, the endpoint is not merely another ambient space containing the same function. It is the exact level of a newform realizing that eigensystem.

Rung 3 What it buys

A canonical modular object

At the start of essay 14, a modular form was a function with a transformation law. At its end, a good-Hecke eigenform carried arithmetic numbers $a_\ell$. This essay removes the two ambiguities still attached to that object:

  • normalization $a_1=1$ removes scalar rescaling;
  • newness supplies the unique exact level.

So the phrase

$$ \text{“a normalized weight-$2$ newform of level $N$”} $$

now names a rigid arithmetic object. Its good coefficients determine it, its bad-prime operators are part of its eigenpacket, and $N$ records where that packet is genuinely new.

The level-$11$ thread is ready to cross the bridge

The same form has now acquired four compatible descriptions:

essaydescription of the same object
12the eta product $(\eta(z)\eta(11z))^2$ and its $q$-expansion
13the unique line in $S_2(\Gamma_0(11))$
14a normalized eigenform with good eigenvalues $a_\ell$
15the normalized newform of exact level $11$

Essay 16 can now attach a Galois representation to this precise object. Essay 17 can attach its $L$-function, including the local factors at bad primes. Essay 18 can state modularity with no ambiguity: an elliptic curve of conductor $N$ corresponds to a weight-$2$ newform of exact level $N$.

The ending has also become sharper

Essay 25 ends by demanding a weight-$2$ newform of level $2$. That demand now means a genuinely new primitive form, not an arbitrary cusp form viewed at level $2$. At level $2$ the distinction happens not to affect the numerical contradiction because the entire cusp space is zero. At level $22$ it matters decisively: the cusp space has dimension $2$, while the newspace has dimension $0$.

This also explains why lists of levels with no newforms are longer than lists of genus-zero modular curves. Newform theory removes inherited dimensions before asking whether anything remains.

Exactly where this essay stops

Newform theory identifies the modular object and its exact level. It does not yet:

  • construct the Galois representation $\rho_{f,p}$;
  • build the Euler product $L(f,s)$ and match its local factors;
  • prove that an elliptic curve supplies such a newform; or
  • lower the level of a residual representation.

Those are essays 16–18 and 25. The gain here is narrower but indispensable: their statements now refer to an object whose normalization and level have actually been defined.

Rung 4 Reading

The boundary inherited from essay 14. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 24, remark 24.16 and the discussion surrounding theorems 24.20–24.21, states both failures used here: the clean Hermitian theory is prime to the level, and at general level the common good-Hecke eigenspaces need not be one-dimensional. Theorem 24.21 gives the resulting normalized newform basis.

The full old/new repair. The 2018 ENS notes Modular Forms, §5.3, define the two degeneracy maps, oldspace and newspace; compute the bad-prime action; introduce the Fricke operator to control adjoints; and state the Atkin–Lehner–Li theorem as theorem 5.13. In particular, a good-Hecke eigenform in the newspace is an eigenform for every $T_m$, two such forms with the same good eigenvalues are scalar multiples, and normalized primitive forms form an orthogonal basis.
