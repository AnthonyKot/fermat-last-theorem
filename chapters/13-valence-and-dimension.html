Part III · Modular forms — Essay 13

The dimension formula

Two facts do all the work: weight two is the weight at which a form becomes a differential, and a covering of the sphere has a genus determined by where it ramifies. Put them together and the four counts of essay 11 become the number the argument ends on.

This essay derives, and imports two things Derives: $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(N))=1+\frac{\mu}{12}-\frac{\nu_2}{4}-\frac{\nu_3}{3}-\frac{\nu_\infty}{2}$ from Riemann–Hurwitz, and evaluates it at $N=2$ and $N=11$ with the ramification written out. Imports: that $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ is the genus of $X_0(N)$, and Riemann–Hurwitz together with the ramification structure of $X_0(N)\to X(1)$. Ledger: Resolves assumption (F) by a derived formula resting on two registered imports.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essay 11 produced the four counts — the index $\mu$, the elliptic-point counts $\nu_2$ and $\nu_3$, and the cusp count $\nu_\infty$ — and stated that $X_0(N)=\mathbb{H}^*/\Gamma_0(N)$ is a compact Riemann surface. Essay 12 defined $S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$, the space whose dimension is wanted, and left a warning: $\nu_\infty$ enters the dimension theory twice, and only one of the two appearances is the $-\nu_\infty/2$ in the formula. This essay is where that gets sorted.

This essay discharges (F). Of the two hypotheses Part III was opened to settle — (F) the dimension formula and (M) modularity — (F) stops being an assumption here. It becomes a derivation resting on the two imports named above, and (M) is left as the single deep input on the modular side. Essay 25's own remaining inputs are (M), Ribet's theorem stated there, and finiteness at $p$ from essay 24: three, down from four.

Rung 2 The construction

Why weight two, finally

Essay 12 defined weight-$k$ forms for every even $k$ and said the Fermat argument wants $k=2$ without saying why. Here is the reason, and it is a one-line differentiation.

A form of weight $k$ satisfies $f(\gamma z)=(cz+d)^kf(z)$. Differentiate the action:

$$ \frac{d(\gamma z)}{dz}=\frac{d}{dz}\frac{az+b}{cz+d} =\frac{a(cz+d)-c(az+b)}{(cz+d)^2}=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}=\frac{1}{(cz+d)^2}, $$

the determinant collapsing the numerator exactly as it did in essay 11. So

$$ f(\gamma z)\,d(\gamma z)=(cz+d)^kf(z)\cdot\frac{dz}{(cz+d)^2} =(cz+d)^{k-2}f(z)\,dz. $$

The factor $(cz+d)^{k-2}$ is $1$ for every $\gamma$ precisely when $k=2$, and for any other weight it genuinely varies with $\gamma$. So

$$ \boxed{\quad k=2\iff f(z)\,dz\text{ is invariant under }\Gamma_0(N).\quad} $$

An invariant differential on $\mathbb{H}$ descends to a differential on the quotient. And the cusp condition from essay 12 — $a_0=0$ at every cusp — is exactly what makes it holomorphic there rather than having a pole. Weight two is not a convention: it is the weight at which modular forms are differential forms on $X_0(N)$.

Imported. The space of holomorphic differentials on a compact Riemann surface of genus $g$ has dimension $g$. Combined with the identification above, $$\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(N))=g\bigl(X_0(N)\bigr).$$ This is the one place the collection needs Riemann surface theory, and it is why the answer will be a genus rather than a count of conditions.

A covering of the sphere

The inclusion $\Gamma_0(N)\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ gives a map of quotients

$$ \pi:X_0(N)\longrightarrow X(1)=\mathbb{H}^*/\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}), $$

and two things about it are already known. Its degree is the index $\mu=[\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}):\Gamma_0(N)]$ from essay 11 — a generic point has $\mu$ preimages, one per coset. And the target is a sphere: $X(1)$ has genus $0$, being parametrised by a single coordinate.

Imported: Riemann–Hurwitz, and where this map ramifies. For a degree-$\mu$ covering of compact Riemann surfaces, $$2g_{X_0(N)}-2=\mu\bigl(2g_{X(1)}-2\bigr)+\sum_P(e_P-1),$$ the sum over points with ramification index $e_P$. For $\pi$ the ramification occurs only above the three special points of $X(1)$ — the elliptic points $i$ and $\rho$ and the cusp — with indices dividing $2$ above $i$, dividing $3$ above $\rho$, and unconstrained above the cusp.

That last clause is where essay 11's counts acquire their meaning, and it is worth being exact about. Above $i$ each preimage has $e=1$ or $e=2$; the ones with $e=1$ are precisely the elliptic points of order $2$ of $\Gamma_0(N)$, of which there are $\nu_2$. The indices sum to $\mu$, so

$$ \nu_2\cdot1+r_2\cdot2=\mu,\qquad r_2=\frac{\mu-\nu_2}{2} $$

points ramify above $i$. Likewise $r_3=(\mu-\nu_3)/3$ points ramify above $\rho$ with $e=3$. Above the cusp there are $\nu_\infty$ preimages, whose indices — the cusp widths — sum to $\mu$.

Both of those divisions must come out whole, which is a real constraint on the four counts and not a formality: the script checks $\mu\equiv\nu_2\ (2)$ and $\mu\equiv\nu_3\ (3)$ for every $N\lt400$.

The computation

Substitute. With $g_{X(1)}=0$ the first term is $-2\mu$, and the ramification sum has three pieces: $r_2$ points contributing $e-1=1$ each, $r_3$ points contributing $2$ each, and the cusps contributing $\sum(e_i-1)=\mu-\nu_\infty$.

$$ \begin{aligned} 2g-2 &=-2\mu+\frac{\mu-\nu_2}{2}+2\cdot\frac{\mu-\nu_3}{3}+\bigl(\mu-\nu_\infty\bigr)\\[.5ex] &=\mu\Bigl(-2+\tfrac12+\tfrac23+1\Bigr)-\frac{\nu_2}{2}-\frac{2\nu_3}{3}-\nu_\infty\\[.5ex] &=\frac{\mu}{6}-\frac{\nu_2}{2}-\frac{2\nu_3}{3}-\nu_\infty, \end{aligned} $$

because $-2+\tfrac12+\tfrac23+1=\tfrac16$. Halve and add one:

$$ \boxed{\quad \dim S_2\bigl(\Gamma_0(N)\bigr)=g\bigl(X_0(N)\bigr) =1+\frac{\mu}{12}-\frac{\nu_2}{4}-\frac{\nu_3}{3}-\frac{\nu_\infty}{2}.\quad} $$

That is assumption (F), now derived. The $12$ in the denominator is $2\times6$, and the $6$ came from four rational numbers adding to $\tfrac16$ — which is the whole reason the formula looks the way it does.

Settling essay 12's warning

Now the point essay 12 flagged and deliberately did not resolve. In the formula above, $\nu_\infty$ appears because the cusps are where the covering ramifies — it is a ramification count, arriving through Riemann–Hurwitz. It has nothing to do with the fact that holomorphy at the cusps imposes $\nu_\infty$ conditions on a $q$-expansion.

That second effect is real and enters elsewhere. For $k\gt2$ the standard dimension formulas give

$$ \dim M_k-\dim S_k=\nu_\infty, $$

which is exactly one condition per cusp, independently imposed — the vanishing conditions counted honestly. The cusp term of $\dim S_k$ carries coefficient $\tfrac k2-1$, so at $k=2$ that coefficient is zero.

It is tempting to stop there and say "so at weight two the conditions are free, which is why $\dim S_2=g$." That is not right, and the arithmetic says so. Substitute $k=2$ into the $k\gt2$ formula and it returns

$$ (2-1)(g-1)+0+0+0\cdot\nu_\infty=g-1, $$

one less than the truth, at every level. Weight two is not a case the general formula covers with a vanishing term; it is a case the general formula misses, and $\dim S_2=g$ has to be stated separately. The reason for the discrepancy is worth naming, because it is the same fact that made this essay work. At weight two $f(z)\,dz$ is a differential, and the sum of the residues of a meromorphic differential on a compact surface is zero. So the $\nu_\infty$ vanishing conditions are not independent — they satisfy exactly one linear relation, and only $\nu_\infty-1$ of them bite:

$$ \dim M_2-\dim S_2=\nu_\infty-1, $$

which the script confirms at every level it checks. At $N=11$: $\dim M_2=2$, $\dim S_2=1$, differing by $1=\nu_\infty-1$, while at weight four $\dim M_4=4$ and $\dim S_4=2$ differ by $2=\nu_\infty$. That missing condition is the $+1$ that turns $g-1$ into $g$.

So $\nu_\infty$ appears twice, for two reasons that are not the same reason, and neither is the other's explanation: once as ramification in the genus, and once as vanishing conditions of which one is redundant at weight two and none is at weight four.

Rung 3 What it buys

Level 2, with the ramification written out

Essay 11 computed $\mu=3$, $\nu_2=1$, $\nu_3=0$, $\nu_\infty=2$. So the covering $X_0(2)\to X(1)$ has degree $3$, and above each special point:

abovepreimagesindices$\sum(e-1)$
$i$$\nu_2=1$ unramified, $r_2=\frac{3-1}{2}=1$ ramified$1,2$$1$
$\rho$$\nu_3=0$ unramified, $r_3=\frac{3-0}{3}=1$ ramified$3$$2$
the cusp$\nu_\infty=2$ cusps$1,2$$1$

Every row's indices sum to $3=\mu$, as they must. Then

$$ 2g-2=-6+1+2+1=-2,\qquad g=0, $$

and therefore

$$ \dim S_2\bigl(\Gamma_0(2)\bigr)=0. $$

That is the ending. Not a formula evaluated to zero but a genus computed from a degree-three covering of the sphere ramified at three points. There is no weight-2 cusp form of level 2 because a surface with that ramification data is a sphere, and a sphere carries no holomorphic differentials.

Level 11, and the form essay 12 wrote down

With $\mu=12$, $\nu_2=\nu_3=0$, $\nu_\infty=2$: six points ramify above $i$, four above $\rho$, and the two cusps have widths summing to $12$ — they are $1$ and $11$. So

$$ 2g-2=-24+6\cdot1+4\cdot2+(12-2)=-24+6+8+10=0,\qquad g=1. $$

A torus, one holomorphic differential, $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(11))=1$. Essay 12 exhibited that differential as $(\eta(z)\eta(11z))^2$ and matched its coefficients to essay 08's point counts. The genus computation is why there was exactly one thing to find.

How much room the ending has

A contradiction that arrives at zero invites the worry that it only just arrives. It does not. The formula gives $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(N))=0$ for

$$ N=1,\dots,10,\ 12,\ 13,\ 16,\ 18,\ 25, $$

and for no other $N$ below $400$ — the classical list of genus-zero levels, which the script reproduces. Level $2$ sits comfortably inside it. Had essay 24's conductor computation returned $3$, or $4$, or $5$, the argument would have closed identically. What matters is not the value $2$ but that level lowering drives the level into a range where the spaces are empty for the unglamorous reason that a sphere has no differentials.

With (F) discharged, essay 25's assumptions are down to (M), modularity, plus Ribet's theorem stated there and finiteness at $p$ from essay 24. Essays 14 and 15 build the vocabulary (M) is phrased in — Hecke operators, and what the level of a newform means — and essays 16 to 18 state (M) itself.

Rung 4 Reading

The dimension in terms of the genus and the counts. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 24, theorem 24.8, gives $\dim M_k(\Gamma)$ and $\dim S_k(\Gamma)$ for a congruence subgroup in terms of $g(X(\Gamma))$, $\nu_2$, $\nu_3$ and $\nu_\infty$, and records the case used here: $\dim S_2(\Gamma)=g(\Gamma)$. Its remark 24.9 notes the relationship between $S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ and the regular differentials on $X_0(N)$, whose dimensions coincide — which is the identification this essay derives from $d(\gamma z)=dz/(cz+d)^2$.

Further reference. Diamond and Shurman's A First Course in Modular Forms derives the genus of $X_0(N)$ from the ramification of $X_0(N)\to X(1)$ and treats the valence formula and the isomorphism between weight-two cusp forms and holomorphic differentials.

Check it yourself. Run checks/dimension_formula.py. It verifies numerically that $d(\gamma z)/dz=1/(cz+d)^2$ over every matrix with entries at most $3$, and that the surviving factor $(cz+d)^{k-2}$ is trivial only at $k=2$; that $\mu-\nu_2$ is even and $\mu-\nu_3$ divisible by $3$ for every $N\lt400$, so the ramification counts are whole; that $-2+\frac12+\frac23+1=\frac16$ exactly, in rational arithmetic; that the Riemann–Hurwitz computation reproduces the closed formula for every $N\lt400$; the explicit ramification data at $N=2$ and $N=11$; and that the vanishing levels are exactly the classical genus-zero list. The two-appearances claim is pinned separately in checks/modular_forms.py, which asserts both halves: that substituting $k=2$ into the $k\gt2$ formula returns $g-1$ rather than $g$, and that $\dim M_2-\dim S_2=\nu_\infty-1$ while $\dim M_k-\dim S_k=\nu_\infty$ for $k\geq4$.
