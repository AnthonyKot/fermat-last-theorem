Part II · Elliptic curves — Essay 07

The group law

A line meets a cubic three times. Declare those three points to sum to zero, and the rational solutions stop being a list: they become an abelian group that geometry can calculate.

This essay proves, states, and computes Derives: the chord-and-tangent operation and its short-Weierstrass formulas. Proves: rational closure and an order-five calculation on the curve from essay 06. States: associativity, the one group axiom not visible from the construction, and complex uniformization $E(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$.

Rung 1 What we already have

Essay 06 gave us a smooth projective cubic in general Weierstrass form,

$$ E:\quad y^2+a_1xy+a_3y=x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6, $$

together with its unique point at infinity $O=[0:1:0]$. The rational points are

$$ E(\mathbb{Q}) =\{\,(x,y)\in\mathbb{Q}^2:\text{$(x,y)$ satisfies the equation}\,\}\cup\{O\}. $$

So far this is only a set. There is no reason yet that two rational points should produce a third, or that $O$ should do anything except close the projective curve. The missing structure comes from the degree: substituting a line into a cubic gives an equation of degree three. If two intersection points are known, the third is forced.

The learner's task in this essay is concrete: given $P,Q\in E(\mathbb{Q})$, calculate $P+Q$ and know which part of the claim has actually been proved. That second clause matters. Most group axioms fall straight out of the picture; associativity does not.

Rung 2 The construction

Three points on a line sum to zero

Take two points $P$ and $Q$ on $E$. Draw the line through them. If $P=Q$, use the tangent line at $P$. The line meets the cubic once more at a point $S$, counting a tangency as a repeated intersection. Define

$$ P+Q=-S. $$

Equivalently, whenever $P,Q,S$ lie on one line, counted with multiplicity, declare

$$ P+Q+S=O. $$

The minus sign needs a definition. At a fixed $x$, the general Weierstrass equation is a quadratic in $y$. Its two roots add to $-(a_1x+a_3)$, so the other point above the same $x$-coordinate is

$$ -(x,y)=(x,-y-a_1x-a_3). $$

Set $-O=O$. The line through $P$ and $O$ is vertical, and its third intersection is $-P$; therefore $P+O=-(-P)=P$. The vertical line through $P$ and $-P$ has $O$ as its third intersection, so $P+(-P)=-O=O$. Thus $O$ is the identity and the displayed point is the inverse of $P$. The construction treats $P$ and $Q$ symmetrically, so $P+Q=Q+P$.

Why rational points stay rational

Suppose $P$ and $Q$ have rational coordinates. Their line has rational coefficients. Substituting it into the equation of $E$ produces a cubic polynomial in $x$ with rational coefficients. Two roots are the rational $x$-coordinates of $P$ and $Q$; Vieta's formulas make the third root rational too. Its $y$-coordinate lies on the same rational line, and negation preserves rationality. Thus

$$ P,Q\in E(\mathbb{Q})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P+Q\in E(\mathbb{Q}). $$

The tangent case is the same argument with a repeated root. Smoothness, supplied by essay 06, guarantees that a tangent is defined; implicit differentiation gives it rational slope at a rational point unless it is vertical, in which case doubling gives $O$.

The formulas in short form

Over $\mathbb{Q}$ we may use a short equation $y^2=x^3+Ax+B$ when we want to calculate. This does not undo essay 06's warning: short form is convenient for the group law over $\mathbb{Q}$, while general integral form remains necessary for arithmetic at $2$ and $3$.

Let $P=(x_1,y_1)$ and $Q=(x_2,y_2)$, with $Q\neq-P$. The slope is

$$ m= \begin{cases} \dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1},&P\neq Q,\\[1.1ex] \dfrac{3x_1^2+A}{2y_1},&P=Q. \end{cases} $$

Then $P+Q=(x_3,y_3)$, where

$$ x_3=m^2-x_1-x_2,\qquad y_3=m(x_1-x_3)-y_1. $$

The first formula is Vieta's relation for the three intersections. The second substitutes $x_3$ into the line and reflects the third point across the $x$-axis. Together with $P+O=P$ and $P+(-P)=O$, these formulas cover every case.

The axiom the picture does not prove

Associativity is stated, not proved. For all $P,Q,R\in E(\mathbb{Q})$, $$(P+Q)+R=P+(Q+R).$$ The identity, inverses, closure and commutativity were established above. Associativity is a theorem about the rational formulas defining addition, and its complete proof must handle tangencies, repeated points and points at infinity simultaneously. We do not reproduce that case analysis here.

With that theorem, $E(\mathbb{Q})$ is an abelian group. The word “abelian” means that the order of addition does not matter. It does not mean the group is finite, or that its rational points are easy to find; both questions are substantially harder than defining the operation.

Rung 3 What it buys

The curve from essay 06 contains a five-cycle

Return to

$$ E:\quad y^2+y=x^3-x^2, $$

and let $P=(0,0)$. For this general Weierstrass equation, $-(x,y)=(x,-y-1)$, so $-P=(0,-1)$.

Double $P$. Implicit differentiation gives

$$ (2y+1)\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-2x, $$

so the tangent at $P$ is $y=0$. Substitution gives $0=x^2(x-1)$: $P$ occurs twice and the third intersection is $(1,0)$. Reflecting it in the Weierstrass sense gives

$$ 2P=(1,-1). $$

Add $P$ once more. The line through $(0,0)$ and $(1,-1)$ is $y=-x$. On the curve,

$$ x^2-x=x^3-x^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x(x-1)^2=0. $$

The third intersection is $(1,-1)$ again, now with multiplicity two. Negating it gives

$$ 3P=(1,0). $$

The line through $P$ and $3P$ is $y=0$, whose third intersection is $P$ again. Therefore

$$ 4P=-P=(0,-1),\qquad 5P=P+(-P)=O. $$

$0$$O$
$1$$(0,0)$
$2$$(1,-1)$

$5$$O$

The five points $O,P,2P,3P,4P$ are distinct, so $P$ has order exactly $5$. This proves that they form a subgroup of $E(\mathbb{Q})$; it does not prove that there are no other rational points. Essay 09 will call such finite-order points torsion and study them systematically.

It is worth flagging what has just been constructed, because a later essay turns on its absence. The subgroup $\langle P\rangle$ is a rational cyclic subgroup of order $5$ — the kernel of a rational $5$-isogeny — and its existence forces the mod $5$ representation of this curve to be reducible, since the Galois action preserves a line. Essay 24's argument needs exactly the opposite for the Frey curve, and gets it from Mazur's isogeny theorem: a semistable curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ admits no such subgroup of order $\ell$ once $\ell$ is large enough. That theorem will arrive in essay 10 as a name; the computation above is an instance of what it forbids, done by hand, so that the name has something to refer to.

The same group becomes a torus over $\mathbb{C}$

A lattice in the complex plane is an additive subgroup

$$ \Lambda=\mathbb{Z}\omega_1+\mathbb{Z}\omega_2, $$

where $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$. Quotienting $\mathbb{C}$ by this lattice identifies opposite sides of a fundamental parallelogram. The result $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ is a torus, and its addition law is simply complex addition modulo the lattice.

Complex uniformization, stated. For every elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{C}$ there is a lattice $\Lambda$ and an isomorphism of complex analytic groups

$$ \Phi:\mathbb{C}/\Lambda\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}E(\mathbb{C}). $$

In one direction the map is explicit: the Weierstrass function $\wp$ attached to $\Lambda$ satisfies a cubic differential equation, and $z\mapsto(\wp(z),\wp'(z))$ lands on the corresponding elliptic curve, with the lattice class of $0$ sent to $O$. The theorem says every complex elliptic curve arises this way and that addition on the torus becomes chord-and-tangent addition on the cubic.

This theorem concerns $E(\mathbb{C})$, not $E(\mathbb{Q})$; rational points form a special arithmetic subset of the torus. But the picture earns its place twice. First, division by $n$ on a two-dimensional lattice produces a two-dimensional grid of points, the shape essay 09 will turn into $E[n]\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$. Second, modular curves in Part IV are also complex curves with group-theoretic structure. A map from one of them to an elliptic curve is no longer a mysterious jump between unrelated objects: analytically, both are complex quotients carrying arithmetic symmetry.

Rung 4 Reading

The group law. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 2, §§2.1–2.3, derives chord-and-tangent addition, gives the short-Weierstrass formulas and checks the group axioms. Its discussion makes the same proof boundary used here explicit: commutativity is immediate, while associativity requires a separate argument.

Lattices and the Weierstrass function. The same course's Fall 2023 Lecture 14, definition 14.1 and §14.4, constructs $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$, the function $\wp$, and the cubic attached to a lattice.

Uniformization. Fall 2023 Lecture 15, theorem 15.1 and corollary 15.12, proves that $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ maps isomorphically as a group to its elliptic curve and that every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{C}$ arises from a lattice.

Further reference. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves develops the group law on a general Weierstrass equation and the arithmetic of rational points.

Check it yourself. Run checks/group_law.py. It implements the general Weierstrass addition formulas with exact rational arithmetic, verifies the five multiples above, and checks the complete addition table of the subgroup they form.
