Part VI · The contradiction — Essay 24

The Frey representation

The curve's conductor is $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, an unknown number of unbounded size. Its mod $p$ representation has conductor $2$. That collapse is one line of arithmetic, and it is the reason the argument can end.

This essay computes, derives, and states Computes: $N(\bar\rho_{E,p})=2$ — every odd prime drops out because $p$ divides its valuation of $\Delta_{\min}$, and $2$ survives because $p\nmid8$. Establishes: $\bar\rho$ irreducible for every prime $p\geq5$, via Mazur's sharpened isogeny theorem — whose three hypotheses are all already proved here — so no exponent is handed to classical work. Derives independently: the same conclusion for $p=5,7$ by an elementary $2$-torsion argument, which reaches every case except $21\mid abc$ and $85\mid abc$ and is kept because it shows why the $2$-torsion is what sharpens Mazur. States: that $\bar\rho$ is finite at $p$, and the ramification criterion for multiplicative primes.

Rung 1 What we already have

Everything. This essay has no forward debts.

Essay 23 is the arithmetic input: the Frey curve is semistable, its minimal model has

$$ \Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}, $$

every bad prime is multiplicative, and its conductor is $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$.

Essay 10 supplies the representation $\bar\rho=\bar\rho_{E,p}:G_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$, the facts that $\det\bar\rho=\bar\chi$ and that every $\bar\rho_{E,\ell}$ is odd, the equivalence reducible $\iff$ a rational subgroup of order $p$, Mazur's isogeny theorem, and Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich.

Essay 09 supplies the extra structure that turns out to matter for small $p$: the Frey curve has full rational $2$-torsion, $E[2]\subset E(\mathbb{Q})$.

Essay 05 supplies the object being computed — the conductor $N(\rho)=\prod\ell^{n_\ell}$, with $n_\ell=0$ exactly when $\rho$ is unramified at $\ell$, and $n_\ell=\dim V-\dim V^{I_\ell}$ when the ramification is tame.

So two of Ribet's four hypotheses are already discharged before this essay begins: $\bar\rho$ is odd, for every elliptic curve at once, and its determinant is known. What remains is irreducibility and the conductor.

Rung 2 The construction

When does the mod $p$ representation forget a bad prime?

There is an apparent contradiction to clear first, and clearing it is the essay.

Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich says $\rho_{E,p}$ is unramified at $\ell\neq p$ exactly when $E$ has good reduction at $\ell$. The Frey curve has bad reduction at every prime dividing $abc$ — an enormous set. So how can $\bar\rho$ be unramified outside $2$?

Because $\bar\rho$ is the representation modulo $p$, and reduction can lose ramification that the $p$-adic representation has. At a prime of multiplicative reduction the local picture is governed by a single integer, $v_\ell(\Delta_{\min})$, and inertia acts through it; passing mod $p$ sees that integer only modulo $p$. So the ramification survives exactly when $p$ does not divide it.

Stated: the ramification criterion at a multiplicative prime. Let $E$ have multiplicative reduction at $\ell\neq p$. Then $$ \bar\rho_{E,p}\text{ is unramified at }\ell \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\mid v_\ell\bigl(\Delta_{\min}(E)\bigr), $$ and when it is ramified the ramification is tame with $n_\ell=1$. Equivalently, in the form Serre gave it: the conductor of $\bar\rho_{E,p}$ for a semistable $E$ is $$ N(\bar\rho_{E,p})=\prod_{\substack{\ell\\ v_\ell(\Delta_{\min})\not\equiv0\ (p)}}\ell. $$

That is the whole mechanism, and it is a local fact about multiplicative reduction, imported here, which converts essay 23's valuations into ramification data. Essay 23 did the work of computing those valuations; this essay only has to read them modulo $p$.

Reading the valuations modulo $p$

From essay 23, $\Delta_{\min}=2^{-8}(abc)^{2p}$. Take valuations.

At an odd bad prime $\ell$, so $\ell\mid abc$ and $\ell\neq2$:

$$ v_\ell(\Delta_{\min})=2p\,v_\ell(abc)\;\equiv\;0\pmod p. $$

A multiple of $p$, because $a$, $b$, $c$ enter as $p$-th powers. Every odd prime drops out of the conductor. This is the first place the $p$-th power structure matters at the odd primes — and that is where the enormous conductor collapses. Essay 23's odd-prime paragraph explicitly did without it, needing only coprimality; but its prime-$2$ analysis did use it, since $v_2(\Delta_{\min})=2p\,v_2(b)-8\geq2p-8$ requires $B=b^p$, and essay 23 called that the second place $p\geq5$ does real work. So this is not the first use of the structure in the collection, only the first at odd primes.

At $2$, using $v_2(abc)=v_2(b)$ since $a$ and $c$ are odd:

$$ v_2(\Delta_{\min})=2p\,v_2(b)-8\;\equiv\;-8\pmod p. $$

And $p\nmid8$, since $p$ is an odd prime. So $-8\not\equiv0$, and $2$ survives. Two facts about $2$ conspire here, both inherited: essay 23's mod-$32$ normalisation is what made $v_2(\Delta_{\min})$ positive at all, so that $2$ is a bad prime; and the subtracted $8$ from the minimal model at $2$ is what stops its valuation being a multiple of $p$. Had the model not been made minimal, this exponent would have been wrong.

Therefore the product has exactly one factor, and since the reduction at $2$ is multiplicative the ramification is tame with $n_2=1$:

$$ \boxed{\quad N(\bar\rho_{E,p})=2.\quad} $$

Look at what has happened to the size of things. The curve's conductor is $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, which depends on a solution nobody has and could be astronomically large. The representation's conductor is $2$, for every $p$ and every hypothetical solution. All the arithmetic of the solution has been absorbed into the phrase "a multiple of $p$".

Irreducible: what Mazur gives, and what $E[2]$ adds

Essay 10 reduced irreducibility to a question about subgroups: $\bar\rho$ is reducible exactly when $E$ has a rational subgroup of order $p$. Mazur's theorem forbids that on a semistable curve for $p\gt7$, and essay 23 supplied semistability. So

$$ p\gt7\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bar\rho\text{ is irreducible.} $$

That is the general semistable statement, and taken alone it leaves $p=5$ and $p=7$ inside Mazur's exceptional list. But Mazur's theorem has a sharpened form, and its hypotheses are exactly the three things this collection has already proved.

Stated: Mazur's isogeny theorem, sharpened. $E/\mathbb{Q}$ admits no rational $p$-isogeny if $p$ satisfies at least one of
  • $p>163$; or
  • $p\geq5$, $\#E(\mathbb{Q})[2]=4$, and the conductor of $E$ is square-free.
The second condition is the one that applies. The Frey curve has $p\geq5$ by essay 22's normalisation; $\#E(\mathbb{Q})[2]=4$ is essay 09's full rational $2$-torsion; and its conductor is square-free by essay 23, which is what semistability means by essay 08. So $\bar\rho$ is irreducible for every $p\geq5$, with no exceptional cases.

That is worth pausing on, because it retires the collection's longest-standing external dependency. Until now $n=5$ and $n=7$ were handed to Dirichlet, Legendre and Lamé, and the ledger carried that as an assumed input. The three hypotheses above were already on the books — proved in essays 09, 22 and 23 for their own reasons, with no thought of this theorem — so the sharpened form costs nothing new and covers both exponents.

The elementary route, for the cases that no longer need it

Before that statement was found, the two exponents could be attacked with the same $2$-torsion structure by hand, and the argument is worth keeping: it is short, it is checkable, and it explains why the $2$-torsion is what sharpens Mazur rather than merely citing that it does.

Since $E[2]\subset E(\mathbb{Q})$ and reduction is injective on $2$-torsion at an odd prime of good reduction, $E[2]$ injects into $E(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$, so

$$ 4\mid\#\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_\ell)=\ell+1-a_\ell,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad a_\ell\equiv\ell+1\pmod4. $$

With the Hasse bound $|a_\ell|\leq2\sqrt\ell$ from essay 08, almost nothing is left at small $\ell$:

If $\bar\rho$ were reducible it would be upper triangular by essay 05, so every Frobenius characteristic polynomial would split over $\mathbb{F}_p$. By essay 10 that polynomial is $x^2-a_\ell x+\ell$, so splitting requires $a_\ell^2-4\ell$ to be a square modulo $p$. One non-square proves irreducibility, and every forced value above is one:

So this route alone gives irreducibility at $p=5$ unless $21\mid abc$, and at $p=7$ unless $85\mid abc$, and no further uniform witness exists below $400$. The sharpened Mazur statement covers those residual cases too, which is why the collection now cites it; but the computation above is what makes the mechanism visible, and it is the reason to expect such a statement to exist.

Finite at $p$

Stated. $\bar\rho_{E,p}$ is finite at $p$ — arising from a finite flat group scheme over $\mathbb{Z}_p$ — which for a semistable curve holds because the $p$-torsion of $E$ is. This is a condition at $p$ itself, separate from everything above, which concerned primes $\ell\neq p$. Ribet's theorem needs it, and this collection does not prove it.

Why the gap Ribet crosses is the size it is

Set the two conductors side by side:

$$ N_E=\operatorname{rad}(abc), \qquad N(\bar\rho_{E,p})=2. $$

The first is a statement about the curve and grows with the solution; the second is a statement about the representation and does not. Modularity, in essay 18, attaches to $E$ a newform of level $N_E$ — useless on its own, because $N_E$ is unknown. Ribet's theorem, in essay 25, says the level can be pushed down to $N(\bar\rho)$. The distance between those two numbers is exactly what level lowering exists to cross, and this essay is where the far side becomes a specific number rather than a hope.

And the number is $2$ for a reason worth restating: not because of anything about level $2$, but because $2$ is the one prime whose valuation of $\Delta_{\min}$ escapes being a multiple of $p$ — and it escapes by exactly the $8$ that the minimal model at $2$ removed.

Rung 4 Reading

Serre's level recipe. Andrew Sutherland's MIT 18.783 Fall 2023 Lecture 25, §25.4, gives the optimal level of an irreducible mod-residual-prime representation attached to a semistable curve as the product of the primes whose $\Delta_{\min}$-valuation is not divisible by the residual prime, and applies it to the Frey curve to obtain level $2$. Its letters are the reverse of this collection's — it writes $\ell$ for the residual prime and $p$ for auxiliary ones — so the formula must be transcribed with the roles swapped, as it is above.

Ramification of the Frey representation. Darmon, Diamond and Taylor's survey Fermat's Last Theorem, p. 8, records that the ramification of the division field is restricted to $2$ and the exponent, with the precise statement at their theorem 2.15 in §2.2; their p. 9 records that Mazur's theorem gives irreducibility for exponent greater than $7$ using semistability.

Mazur's exceptional list. The same Lecture 25, §25.4, states that no elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ admits a rational isogeny of prime degree outside $\{2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19,37,43,67,163\}$, of which $19$, $43$, $67$ and $163$ require complex multiplication.

Further reference. Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves treats the Tate curve and the local structure at a prime of multiplicative reduction, which is what the ramification criterion rests on.

Check it yourself. Run checks/frey_conductor.py. It evaluates Serre's recipe symbolically over many exponents and valuation patterns and confirms the product is always exactly $2$; asserts that $p\nmid8$ is what makes $2$ survive; and then computes the irreducibility witnesses by enumerating every $a_\ell$ that full rational $E[2]$ and the Hasse bound permit, rather than sampling curves — confirming $\ell=3,7$ for $p=5$ and $\ell=5,17$ for $p=7$, that no further uniform witness exists below $400$, and hence the exact residual cases $21\mid abc$ and $85\mid abc$.
