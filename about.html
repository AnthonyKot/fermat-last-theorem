Method

About this collection

What these essays prove, what they only state, and how each claim was checked. The distinction is the point, so it comes first.

What is proved, and what is not

A series like this can mislead in one specific way: by narrating a proof so smoothly that a reader finishes believing they have seen one. Three registers are used here, and every essay declares which one it is in.

Proved in full

Fermat's Last Theorem for $n = 4$, via the stronger statement that $x^4+y^4=z^2$ has no solution; the reduction of the general case to odd prime exponents; the first case of $n = 3$ in the Eisenstein integers; the failure of unique factorisation; the construction of the Frey curve from a hypothetical solution and the computation of its discriminant; its conductor and semistability; the conductor of its mod $p$ Galois representation; and $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(2)) = 0$. Given the theorems below, the final contradiction is complete and checkable.

One case is deliberately not complete, and is marked as such where it appears: the second case of $n = 3$, where the ramified prime divides one of the terms, needs a genuine descent rather than a congruence. Essay 02 gives its skeleton faithfully and compresses the analysis of units inside it. Everything else in the list above is done line by line.

Stated with hypotheses, not proved

The modularity theorem (Wiles and Taylor, 1995, for semistable curves; Breuil, Conrad, Diamond and Taylor, 2001, in general); Ribet's level-lowering theorem (1990); Mazur's isogeny theorem; the Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich criterion; Eichler–Shimura; Langlands–Tunnell. Each is stated with its hypotheses spelled out, so a reader can see precisely what is being assumed and check that the Frey curve satisfies it.

Described, not proved

Part V. Wiles's proof of modularity is a long, technical paper, and three essays cannot prove it. What they can do is say what the objects are — deformation rings, Hecke algebras — and what the strategy is. Those essays carry a standing notice to that effect.

So the honest description of this collection is: a complete and checkable derivation of Fermat's Last Theorem from the modularity theorem, plus a guided tour of why modularity is true. The first part you can verify yourself. The second you are asked to take on trust, with the trust located precisely.

The ledger

Every essay ends with a two-column ledger: what it discharged, and what the final contradiction is still owed. The owed column carries forward from essay to essay, and the last essay's must be empty.

This is not decoration. The failure mode of a long expository chain is an essay that leans on something a later essay builds — a defect no careful reading catches, because each essay is internally correct. The ledger makes that condition mechanical: a script checks that the owed column carries forward correctly and that no essay uses a result before the essay that establishes it. It caught one such gap while the series was still being planned.

Reading order

The essays were not written in reading order. The two ends of the rope came first — Fermat's descent and the closing contradiction — so that every construction in between had a stated destination to justify itself against. Read them in numerical order; that order is the dependency order, which is what the ledger enforces.

Sources, and a limitation stated plainly

This collection was written without a personal library of the standard textbooks. Rather than cite section numbers from memory — which is how expository errors are manufactured — the citations are tiered.

Cited precisely: entries in the LMFDB by their stable labels, and published papers and surveys by theorem or section number. Labels and paper theorem numbers do not drift between editions, which is exactly the failure that makes a recalled textbook section number untrustworthy. The standing reference for the architecture of Wiles's proof is the expository survey of Darmon, Diamond and Taylor.

Cited by topic only: textbooks not consulted directly — Silverman's The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves, Diamond and Shurman's A First Course in Modular Forms, and the Cornell–Silverman–Stevens volume. These are named with the topic to look up and no section number, and are marked unverified where they appear. A topic pointer degrades gracefully across editions; a fabricated section number simply sends the reader to the wrong page.

Not cited at all: encyclopaedias, blogs, and lecture videos. They are useful for finding out what a thing is called. They are not evidence.

Numbers are computed, not asserted

Because the usual safety net of a verified page reference is unavailable, every numerical claim is computed from scratch by a script kept alongside the essays, and where possible confirmed a second way. The point counts of a curve are found by brute force and compared against an independently computed $q$-expansion and against the LMFDB entry. The dimension formula for $S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ is evaluated from the index and the counts of elliptic points and cusps, and cross-checked against the classical list of levels for which it vanishes. A claim carried by two independent computations and a database is on firmer ground than one carried by a page number.

Original prose

All exposition here is written from understanding, not adapted from a source. No passage, derivation or figure from any book, paper or transcript is reproduced. The content of a theorem belongs to everyone; its author's sentences do not. Worked examples are constructed for these essays rather than borrowed.

Status

Four of twenty-five essays are written: 01 and 02 (Fermat's descent, $n = 4$ and $n = 3$, and the failure of unique factorisation), and 22 and 25 (the Frey curve, and the closing contradiction). The remaining twenty-one are listed on the contents page with what each will add. Parts II through V are unwritten, so the ledger does not yet close: essay 25 currently states what it depends on rather than pointing back to established essays, and it says so.

This is the third in a series of collections built the same way, after The Quantum Quartet (four authors on early quantum mechanics) and The Bridge (a quantum-mechanics course carried forward to quantum information).
